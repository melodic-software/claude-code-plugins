# Five Compositional Elements

Pat Pattison — Coursera "Songwriting: Writing the Lyrics" specialization
framing, synthesized from *Songwriting: Essential Guide to Lyric Form and
Structure* (1991) Chapter 1-4 (where each element is named separately) and
*Writing Better Lyrics* (2009) Chapter 20-21 ("Form Follows Function", "Great
Balancing Act").

Use this as a section-by-section diagnostic checklist. Every lyric section
(verse, chorus, bridge, refrain, transitional bridge) has these five
characteristics. Naming and inspecting each one surfaces prosody choices
the writer made unconsciously.

## Core idea

A lyric section is the sum of five compositional choices. Each can be set
deliberately for prosody or left to drift. The drift is where most
prosody mismatches hide.

The five elements:

1. **Number of lines**
2. **Length of lines (phrase length in syllables and stresses)**
3. **Rhyme scheme**
4. **Rhyme types** (the stability of each rhyme pair)
5. **Rhythm** (the stress pattern under the line)

> "There will be those five characteristics." — Pat (Coursera companion)

The five are not a rule; they are the five places where craft choices
land. A section is "tight" when all five support the central emotion and
the section's job within the form.

## Why all five at once

The classic prosody mistake is to fix one element in isolation. Switching
a rhyme type from family to assonance can change a section's stability,
but if the line lengths and rhythm still push stable, the new rhyme just
sounds wrong. Diagnose all five before changing any one.

## The diagnostic worksheet

For each section, fill in the five rows:

```text
Section: <verse 1 | chorus | bridge | etc.>

1. Number of lines:    <count>
2. Line lengths:       <stresses per line, e.g., 4/3/4/3 or 4/4/4/4>
3. Rhyme scheme:       <letters, e.g., abab, aabb, abcb, xaxa, none>
4. Rhyme types:        <per pair: perfect / family / additive / subtractive
                        / assonance / consonance / partial / weak-syllable>
5. Rhythm:             <duple / triple / mixed; established pattern;
                        deliberate variations>

Verdict:
- Supports central emotion? <yes | partially | no>
- Supports section job in form? <yes | partially | no>
- Strongest element: <which>
- Weakest element: <which>
- Cheapest fix: <one move>
```

Run the worksheet per section. Compare sections side by side. The
contrasts and the consistencies are the form.

## Each element in turn

### 1. Number of lines

Even counts (2, 4, 6) feel balanced. Odd counts (3, 5) feel unbalanced
and push forward. A chorus that needs to arrive often uses an even
number. A verse that needs to move usually does too — but a verse with
an extra phrase or fewer phrases than the chorus creates contrast.

See [phrasing](phrasing.md) "number of phrases" for *Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure* (1991), Chapter 1.

### 2. Line lengths

Stresses per line, not syllables. Common Meter is 4/3/4/3. Tetrameter
is 4/4/4/4. Pentameter is 5/5/5/5. Mixed line lengths create motion
(longer line setups, shorter line punchlines, or the reverse).

Length controls speed in the listener's ear: shorter feels faster.

See [phrasing](phrasing.md) "length of phrases" for *Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure* (1991), Chapter 2 and
[meter](meter.md) for stress-count discipline.

### 3. Rhyme scheme

The letters describing which lines rhyme with which. Adjacent rhymes
(aabb) close fast; alternating (abab) suspend across the pair;
enclosed (abba) wrap; unrhymed (xaxa or xxxa) open. Mixed schemes
(abcb) close some pairs while leaving others open.

See [rhyme strategy](rhyme-strategy.md) for scheme as a control of
pace, flow, and closure.

### 4. Rhyme types

The stability of each rhyme. Perfect closes hardest; family closes
nearly as hard; additive / subtractive close meaningfully; assonance
and consonance suspend; partial opens. A section can have multiple
rhyme types across its scheme.

See [rhyme types](rhyme-types.md) for the full stability scale and
worked examples per type.

### 5. Rhythm

The pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables. Duple (one stress
per two syllables), triple (one stress per three syllables), or
mixed. Once established, the pattern carries the listener's
expectation. Deliberate variation creates motion, but distortion of
natural word stress breaks meaning.

See [meter](meter.md) for the Structural Pentad (balance, pace, flow,
closure, type of closure) and Paradigm One/Two/Three patterns.

## Reading the worksheet

The diagnostic is not "are the elements consistent". Consistency
across a section is not the goal; coherence with the section's job
is. A chorus typically wants:

- even line count (balance),
- arrival-shaped line lengths (often longer-then-shorter, or all
  same),
- a closed rhyme scheme (often couplet or abcb with a closed final
  pair),
- mostly perfect or family rhyme (closure),
- a regular established rhythm.

A verse typically wants:

- variable line count is fine if motion is wanted,
- mixed line lengths to push forward,
- an alternating or open scheme,
- mixed rhyme types (family or additive at line-end positions, or
  remote types for openness),
- a rhythm that sets up the chorus rhythm by contrast.

These are defaults. The five-row check exposes when the writer
diverged from default — and lets the writer decide whether the
divergence was deliberate prosody or accidental drift.

## Worked example

A four-line verse with three lines feeling right and one feeling
"off" — the five-row check might return:

```text
Section: verse 1

1. Number of lines:    4
2. Line lengths:       4/4/4/4 stresses
3. Rhyme scheme:       abab
4. Rhyme types:        perfect / family / consonance / family
5. Rhythm:             duple, established line 1, deliberate
                       variation line 3 (matches narrative pivot)

Verdict:
- Supports central emotion? Partially — central emotion is
  resigned-stable; consonance on line 3 opens suspended.
- Supports section job? Yes for setup, no for arrival.
- Strongest element: rhythm variation at line 3 (matches pivot).
- Weakest element: consonance rhyme type at line 3 fights the
  resigned tone.
- Cheapest fix: change line 3's rhyme type from consonance to family.
```

One row fix. The other four stay. Diagnose all five, change one.

## Coaching prompts

- For each section: fill the five rows. Do not skip rows. Even "no
  rhyme" is an answer for rows 3 and 4.
- Which row is doing the most work in this section?
- Which row is the weakest?
- If you changed one row, which fix is cheapest?
- Are the sections' rows consistent with their jobs (verse setup,
  chorus arrival, bridge release)?
- Where do two adjacent sections agree on all five rows? That spot
  may need contrast.

## Anti-patterns

- **Filling rows from memory without re-reading the section.** Read
  aloud first, fill the worksheet from what you heard.
- **Treating consistency across sections as a virtue.** Verse and
  chorus should differ on at least two rows.
- **Changing all five rows at once.** One row at a time. Then re-test.
- **Skipping row 4 (rhyme types).** This is the row most writers leave
  on autopilot. Naming it forces a stability decision.
- **Counting syllables instead of stresses for row 2.** Stress count
  is what the ear tracks.

## Four-juggling-balls origin (*Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure* (1991) introduction)

Pat's *Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure* (1991) introduction frames lyric craft as juggling balls — the
writer learns one ball before adding the next. In *Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure* (1991), four
balls (not five) are introduced sequentially:

- Ball 1 (Chapter 1) — phrase count (number of lines)
- Ball 2 (Chapter 2) — phrase length (length of lines)
- Ball 3 (Chapter 3) — rhythm
- Ball 4 (Chapter 4) — rhyme scheme

The **fifth element (rhyme type)** was added in *Essential Guide to Rhyming* (2014) (2014). *Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure* (1991)
intentionally uses only **perfect rhyme and identity** for all 44
exercises — no family, additive, assonance, or consonance vocabulary.

This pedagogical bounding matters: *Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure* (1991) exercises should be coached
WITHOUT rhyme-type substitutions because the bounding is intentional.
Pat wants the writer to feel the first four levers fully before adding
the fifth.

When running *Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure* (1991) exercises on a writer, hold the rhyme-type pass for
later. Apply only perfect rhyme + identity within *Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure* (1991) drills, then
re-do with full type vocabulary once the four-ball foundation is solid.
