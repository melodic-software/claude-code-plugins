## Core idea

Repetition is productive only when the repeated words mean more the next time
they arrive. The words may stay the same, but the listener's information,
perspective, or emotional pressure must change.

> Put separate ideas in separate boxes.

A repeated line, refrain, chorus, hook, or title should gather weight from each
new section. If the sections only restate the same idea, repetition becomes
stagnant and the song loses energy.

## Repaintable repetition

Chapter 9 adds a second requirement: the repeated material must be capable of
receiving new colors. Even strong verse development can fail if a refrain or
chorus is locked into one tense or point of view.

The repeated section is color-resistant when:

- its verb tense conflicts with later verses,
- its pronouns lock it to one speaker or character,
- it explains too much before the verse can color it,
- it answers the question too early,
- it cannot be sung unchanged after a new verse angle.

When the repeat resists recoloring, strip it.

## Stripping tense

Verbs determine tense. To make a refrain or chorus tense-neutral, use one of
three strategies:

| Strategy | Example shape | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `-ing` form | falling | Accepts past, present, or future context. |
| Infinitive | to fall | Points toward action without committing to time. |
| No verb | a fall | Turns the repeat into a commentary or image. |

Try all three before deciding. The goal is not grammar cleverness; the goal is
to let each verse establish its own time while the repeated line remains
singable.

## Stripping point of view

Pronouns determine point of view. A repeated line with `I`, `you`, `he`, `she`,
`we`, or `they` may block a later verse from changing perspective. Remove the
pronoun when the repeated material needs to accept multiple points of view.

Often the same rewrite can strip both tense and POV:

```text
I fell too hard
falling too hard
to fall too hard
too hard a fall
```

Each neutral version lets the verse supply who fell and when.

## Verses show, chorus tells

Chapter 9 states a useful rule of thumb:

> verses show, chorus tells.

Use this carefully. It does not mean choruses should become generic. It means
verses usually carry specific situation, image, and action, while the chorus or
refrain can make a broader statement that the verses keep recoloring.

If the chorus tells too specifically, it may block the verses. If it tells too
generically, it may become cliche. Strip tense and POV without stripping image,
sound, or emotional pressure.

## Productive ambiguity

Neutral repeated material can create productive ambiguity. If a refrain has no
verb or pronoun, several implied readings can coexist. Different verses can
make the repeat seem to refer to the speaker, the listener, another character,
or a whole group.

Use this when ambiguity deepens the song. Avoid it when the story needs a clear
speaker, target, or timeline.

## Chorus stripping workflow

When a repeated section fails after a new verse:

1. Mark all finite verbs in the chorus or refrain.
2. Mark all pronouns.
3. Test whether tense or POV conflicts with any verse.
4. Rewrite key lines using `-ing`, infinitive, or no-verb forms.
5. Remove pronouns where the verse can supply the speaker or target.
6. Keep enough concrete language to avoid blandness.
7. Sing the same repeated text after each verse.
8. Choose the version that accepts the most useful colors.

## The box model

Think of a song as a stack of connected boxes. Each box contains one major idea
or angle plus the repeated element attached to it.

```text
Box 1: first angle + repeated element
Box 2: new angle + repeated element
Box 3: final / heaviest angle + repeated element
```

The later boxes should contain the earlier boxes' weight plus new information.
The final box should feel heaviest.

Ask:

- What does box 1 teach the listener?
- What genuinely new information does box 2 add?
- What does box 3 reveal, resolve, reverse, or deepen?
- Does the repeated line gain meaning each time?
- If the chorus comes back unchanged, has the listener changed enough to hear
  it differently?

## Stagnant repetition

Repetition is stagnant when each verse says essentially the same thing in
different words. Strong imagery cannot fully fix stagnant development; it can
only decorate it.

Symptoms:

- Verse summaries are interchangeable.
- The chorus means the same thing every time.
- The second chorus feels like a replay instead of a deepening.
- The bridge is doing work the verse should have done.
- The title arrives with less force than before.
- The writer is relying on the hook to rescue idle verses.

Fix stagnant repetition by changing the development, not by changing the
chorus words first.

## Separate jobs

Each verse or verse/refrain system should have its own job. Possible divisions:

- perspective: you, I, we,
- time: past, present, future,
- plot: setup, complication, consequence,
- emotional logic: claim, evidence, cost,
- relationship angle: speaker, listener, third party,
- scale: private moment, public consequence, final insight,
- metaphor development: literal object, broader situation, transformed meaning.

If two boxes do the same job, merge, cut, reorder, or rewrite.

## Do not always ask "what next"

When a writer is stuck after a first verse, the next useful question may be:

```text
Where did I get here from?
```

The verse first written may belong in the middle or end. Try moving boxes
around before assuming the song must continue forward from the first draft.

## Chorus and refrain weight

A chorus or refrain can stay textually stable if each preceding section changes
how the listener hears it. This is why verse development matters: the verse
controls the angle of entry into the repeated material.

Use this test for every repeated section:

1. Write a one-sentence summary of each preceding verse.
2. Read the repeated section after each summary.
3. Name the new meaning or pressure the repetition gains.
4. If the answer is "none," rewrite the verse job or cut the repeated return.

Changing chorus words every time can solve one problem while creating another.
If the repeated section is supposed to invite listener participation, too much
change can weaken its chorus function. Develop the boxes before abandoning the
repeat.

## Cutting a weak repeat

Sometimes the right move is to skip a repeated chorus and go directly to a
bridge or contrasting section. This can work when the next chorus would not
gain enough weight yet, but a bridge can add the missing information or give
the music room to breathe.

Use this when:

- a repeated section sags,
- the story needs one more turn before payoff,
- the bridge supplies contrast and new weight,
- the final chorus becomes stronger because the weak middle chorus is gone.

This is a formal risk, but Chapter 6 treats it as a valid toolbox move.

## Productive repetition inside lines

Repetition also works at the line level. Look for smaller pieces inside a line
that can be isolated and repeated to create a new question, command, emphasis,
or emotional turn.

The key is that the repeated fragment must add meaning, not merely echo sound.

## Hidden questions

Interrogative lines can hide smaller questions:

```text
Who do you love? Do you love?
Where did you go? Did you go?
How will I know? Will I know?
```

The repeated fragment changes the energy. The first question asks for an
object, place, reason, or method. The smaller repeated question asks whether
the action exists at all.

Use this with:

- do / did / will,
- can / could / should / would,
- who / what / when / where / why / how.

## Hidden commands

Declarative second-person lines can hide commands:

```text
You tell me the truth.
Tell me the truth.
Truth.
```

A question can also hide a command:

```text
Will you stay?
Stay.
```

This works best in first or second person with present-tense or infinitive
verbs. Third person often creates simple repetition rather than command because
the verb form changes.

## Repetition diagnosis

When reviewing a draft:

1. Mark every repeated line, title, hook, refrain, and chorus.
2. Write the box summary before each repeat.
3. Ask what the repeat gains each time.
4. Identify any boxes doing the same job.
5. Separate ideas by perspective, tense, plot, or emotional function.
6. Reorder boxes if the first-written verse is not the true first verse.
7. Consider cutting a weak middle repeat if it cannot gain weight.
8. Search individual lines for hidden questions or commands.

## Chapter 6 exercise as coaching prompt

Exercise 11 - Three refrains, increasing weight:

- Write three verses.
- End each verse with the same refrain line.
- Give each verse/refrain system a distinct job.
- Move from one image-world or story stage to a heavier one.
- Use research, object writing, and metaphor to make the repeated refrain gain
  weight.
- Make the third box the heaviest.

Exercise 13 - Neutralize repetition:

- Start with a tense- and POV-locked refrain.
- Make one `-ing` version.
- Make one infinitive version.
- Make one no-verb or noun-phrase version.
- Then neutralize a whole chorus so each line avoids fixed tense and POV.
- Let the verses establish who, when, and what changed.

## Skill workflow

When applying this file:

1. Ask the user for the repeated line, chorus, refrain, or hook.
2. Ask for one-sentence summaries of the sections before each repeat.
3. Identify whether the boxes gain weight.
4. If not, propose new verse jobs before rewriting the repeated section.
5. Preserve repeated words when they can gain meaning through development.
6. Cut or delay a repeat when the song has not earned it yet.
7. Strip tense or POV when the repeat cannot accept new verse colors.
8. Add line-level repetition only when the fragment changes sentence type,
   emphasis, or meaning.
9. Check final prosody against form, rhyme, rhythm, and section contrast.

## Named verse-development formulas (*Writing Better Lyrics* (2009), Chapter 6)

Pat gives two named shortcuts for distributing verse jobs across the boxes
in a multi-verse song. Both work by ensuring each verse has its own
exclusive job — no two verses share the same angle.

### You-I-We formula

Each verse takes a different relational POV:

- **You** — verse focuses on the other person; their action, presence, absence
- **I** — verse focuses on the speaker; the speaker's response, state
- **We** — verse focuses on the shared frame; what we are, were, could be

Any reordering: I → You → We, We → I → You, You → I → We. The formula is
the distribution, not the order.

### Past-Present-Future formula

Each verse takes a different tense:

- **Past** — what happened (the seed event)
- **Present** — what is now (current state)
- **Future** — what will be / could be (projection or fear)

Reverse-chronology orderings are strong plays — Future → Present → Past
makes the listener reconstruct backwards.

### Combining the formulas

The two formulas can stack: verse 1 = You/Past, verse 2 = I/Present, verse
3 = We/Future. The combined matrix lets one short distribution carry the
song's full emotional arc.

## Hidden questions and hidden commands (*Writing Better Lyrics* (2009), Chapter 6) — grammatical detail

Pat's line-level repetition technique creates short repaintings inside a
single section by deleting grammatical scaffolding. The deleted element
isolates the question or command underneath.

### Hidden questions — full grammatical matrix

A question with an auxiliary verb can be reduced to a hidden sub-question
by deleting the auxiliary:

| Original (full question) | Hidden (auxiliary deleted) | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Who do you love? | Do you love? | Sub-question isolates the verb |
| Where did you go? | Did you go? | Tense + question, narrowed |
| What will you say? | Will you say? | Future modal, narrowed |
| Can you remember? | You remember? | Modal stripped, declarative emerges |
| Should I leave? | I leave? | First-person modal stripped |
| Could she stay? | She stay? | Third-person modal stripped |
| Would they listen? | They listen? | Conditional → declarative |

The technique scales across tenses (do/did/will), modals (can/could/should/would),
and persons (1st/2nd/3rd). The deletion creates a fragment that the listener
hears as both repetition AND new content.

### Hidden commands — subject deletion

A declarative with explicit subject can be reduced to a command by deleting
the subject:

| Original (declarative) | Hidden command (subject deleted) | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| You tell me. | Tell me. | Imperative emerges |
| You want me. | Want me. | Imperative + emotional appeal |
| You hold me. | Hold me. | Imperative + intimacy |

Note: **third-person cannot generate commands** because English third-
person verbs take an -s ("he/she tells"). The deletion only works in
first/second person where the verb base is bare.

### Infinitive isolation — past and future

Pat's extension: the technique works with infinitive verb phrases too.

| Original | Infinitive isolation | Tense effect |
|---|---|---|
| Did you want to win my heart? | Win my heart. | Past → present command |
| Will you say it out loud? | Say it out loud. | Future → present command |
| Did you mean to leave? | Mean to leave? | Past → present sub-question |

The infinitive form lets the same surface fragment work across tenses —
useful when a chorus must remain tense-neutral while verses change tense.

### When to use

Hidden questions / commands work in **repaintable choruses** especially —
the section's surface words stay constant; the verses around each chorus
visit shift what the hidden question / command means in context.
