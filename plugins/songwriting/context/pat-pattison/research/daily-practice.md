## Core idea

The practice skill is a training sequence, not a drafting guarantee.
It builds fast sensory access first, then metaphor-making through controlled
collisions. The practice is short on purpose: the writer should finish with
raw material to mine, not with a polished lyric to defend.

> Sense-bound writing turns observers into participants.

Route practice requests by need:

- For dull, abstract, or under-imaged writing, use
  [object writing](object-writing.md).
- For predictable comparisons or a lack of figurative pressure, use
  [metaphor](metaphor.md).
- For a daily prompt, choose the appropriate day type below and generate one
  focused exercise.

## Practice session shape

Each session has four parts:

1. Prompt: give one seed or collision.
2. Timer: specify 90 seconds, 5 minutes, or 10 minutes.
3. Write: keep the writer inside the exercise while the timer runs.
4. Mine: after the timer, mark the usable images, verbs, titles, metaphor
   seeds, and body details.

Do not critique during the timed write. Diagnosis belongs to the mining phase.

## Days 1-14: object writing

Challenge 1 trains sense-bound access across four categories.

| Days | Category | Timers | Coaching aim |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1-5 | What | 5 minutes, 10 minutes, 90 seconds | Enter through things, events, and scenes. |
| 6-8 | Who | 5 minutes, 10 minutes, 90 seconds | Find character through body, gesture, and want. |
| 9-11 | When | 5 minutes, 10 minutes, 90 seconds | Let time, season, and occasion color the senses. |
| 12-14 | Where | 5 minutes, 10 minutes, 90 seconds | Use place to organize action and pressure. |

Object-writing rules:

- Use sight, hearing, smell, taste, touch, organic sense, and kinesthetic sense.
- Follow sensory association even when it leaves the seed.
- Avoid rhyme, meter, lyric polish, explanation, and theme statements.
- Stop exactly when the timer ends.
- Mine after the buzzer.

Useful prompt families:

- What: mirror, arrow, movie theater, broken cup, rain cloud, bus ticket.
- Who: waitress, priest, cyclist, exhausted drummer, child in a hallway.
- When: first snowfall, late evening, graduation, six in the morning.
- Where: hotel bar, city bus, cliff by the ocean, old church, parking lot.

## Days 15-28: metaphor

Challenge 2 trains metaphor by making collisions, explaining them briefly, and
then using each collision as a 90-second object-writing prompt.

> A metaphor is a collision between ideas

| Days | Practice | Coaching aim |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 15 | Adjective-noun collisions | Make arbitrary collisions meaningful. |
| 16 | Find nouns from adjectives | Keep the adjective literally false. |
| 17 | Find adjectives from nouns | Add friction instead of decoration. |
| 18 | Noun-verb collisions | Use verbs to energize nouns. |
| 19 | Find verbs from nouns | Give static nouns surprising action. |
| 20 | Find nouns from verbs | Find a subject or object for strange action. |
| 21 | Expressed identity | Test noun-noun pairs in all three forms. |
| 22 | Reversed expressed identity | Reverse the nouns and compare pressure. |
| 23 | Find nouns from nouns | Search new identities from a target noun. |
| 24 | Playing in keys | Borrow terms through linking qualities. |
| 25 | Playing in keys | Repeat target-to-quality-to-family practice. |
| 26 | Find linking qualities | List qualities, then ask what else has them. |
| 27 | Find linking qualities | Apply the search to social or abstract terms. |
| 28 | Simile | Write three similes plus elaborations. |

Metaphor-practice rules:

- First write a sentence or short paragraph that makes the collision coherent.
- Then do a 90-second object write from the collision.
- Keep comparisons that are literally false but emotionally legible.
- Prefer collisions that open a family of nouns, verbs, textures, and actions.
- Use simile when the first term should stay in focus.
- Use metaphor when the second term should become the image-world.

## Days 29-42: object writing with metaphor

Challenge 3 combines the first two challenges. It asks the writer to find a
linking quality between two ideas, then do ten-minute object writing through
the lens of the metaphor.

> find some quality that two separate ideas share in common

The basic chain is:

```text
First idea -> linking quality -> target idea -> ten-minute object write
```

The object write should stay sense-bound, but it can carry a more unified
narrative thread than the earlier free-association drills. The writer is no
longer just gathering sensory ore; they are testing whether a metaphor can
keep producing images, actions, and turns.

Challenge 3 daily arc:

| Days | Practice | Direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 29-31 | Linking qualities to target ideas | Supplied qualities, one direction. |
| 32-35 | Working both directions | Supplied qualities, then reverse the lens. |
| 36-39 | Find linking qualities | Writer finds qualities, one direction. |
| 40-42 | Find linking qualities both ways | Writer finds qualities, then reverses. |

Day prompts:

| Day | Prompt | Practice focus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 29 | Snowstorm | Link supplied qualities to target ideas. |
| 30 | Deep-Sea Diver | Explore target ideas through a source lens. |
| 31 | Guitar Solo | Notice how shared qualities open families. |
| 32 | Sleeping Late | Reverse the metaphor after the first write. |
| 33 | Broken Glass | Test whether the linking quality is essential. |
| 34 | Falling in Love | Let strong shared qualities turn both ways. |
| 35 | Cheating Lover | Use danger and damage as metaphor engines. |
| 36 | Magnifying Glass | Find three linking qualities, one direction. |
| 37 | Swimming Hole | Find qualities and target ideas, one direction. |
| 38 | Afternoon Nap | Search qualities before target ideas. |
| 39 | Traffic Cop | Find qualities, then begin reversing again. |
| 40 | Wheelchair | Move both directions from writer-found qualities. |
| 41 | Sailboat | Reverse through motion and grace families. |
| 42 | Vacation | Reverse through abstract and relational targets. |

## Linking quality workflow

Use this workflow when a user has an image and wants it to become more than
description:

1. Name the first idea: object, scene, person, time, or place.
2. List interesting qualities that belong naturally to its family.
3. For each quality, ask what else has that quality.
4. Choose one target idea that creates emotional or narrative pressure.
5. Write for ten minutes about the target idea through the lens of the first
   idea.
6. Mine the write for borrowed language: nouns, verbs, textures, sounds,
   actions, and body detail from the first idea's family.

Example frame:

```text
First idea: <snowstorm>
Linking quality: <cold>
Target idea: <your sneer>
Write: describe the target idea through the source lens for 10 minutes.
```

## Reversing the lens

The middle and final sections of Challenge 3 add reversal. After writing the
target idea through the source lens, turn around and write the source idea
through the target lens.

Use reversal only when the linking quality is essential enough to both ideas.
If the metaphor only shares a thin trait, reversal will feel forced; keep it as
a one-direction metaphor or convert it to simile.

Reversal prompt:

```text
First pass:
<target idea> through the lens of <first idea>

Second pass:
<first idea> through the lens of <target idea>
```

Reversal is useful for:

- testing whether a metaphor is durable,
- discovering unexpected verbs,
- finding bridge or second-verse development,
- escaping a one-line comparison,
- deciding whether the metaphor should organize a section.

If both directions produce strong sensory language, the metaphor can probably
carry more song weight. If only one direction works, keep the useful direction
and do not force symmetry.

## Days 43-56: writing in rhythm and rhyme

Challenge 4 links the practice habit to lyric craft. The writer keeps using
sense-bound object writing and metaphor, but now the raw material must fit a
metrical and rhyme structure.

The full sequence:

| Day | Form | Prompt set |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 43 | Tetrameter lines, no rhyme | Sunset; Art Museum |
| 44 | Tetrameter lines, no rhyme | Digging for Gold; Pickup Truck |
| 45 | Tetrameter couplets | Mountain; Snowstorm |
| 46 | Tetrameter couplets | Train; Sleeping Late |
| 47 | Tetrameter couplets | John Brown; Broken Glass |
| 48 | Tetrameter couplets | Skydiving; Rocking Chair on the Front Porch at Sunset |
| 49 | Common meter `xaxa` | Whistling; Falling in Love |
| 50 | Common meter `xaxa` | Ballerina; 18-Wheeler |
| 51 | Common meter `abab` | Ocean Waves; Magnifying Glass |
| 52 | Common meter `abab` | Slot Machine; Deep-Sea Diver |
| 53 | Six-line `aabccb` / `xxaxxa` | War Zone; Wildflowers |
| 54 | Six-line `abcabc` / `xxaxxa` | Morning Walk; Traffic Cop |
| 55 | Six-line `abcabc` / `xabxab` | Trash Collector; Mowing the Lawn |
| 56 | Unstable `abba` / `axxa` | Cloudy Day; Sleeping Late |

Session rules:

- Keep the writing sense-bound.
- Keep looking for metaphor.
- Count stresses before counting syllables.
- Honor natural word stress, especially secondary stress.
- Use the full timer even if the final form is incomplete.
- Mine after the write for lines that are both vivid and structurally useful.

Challenge 4 is the bridge from practice into song drafting. The output may be
usable lyric material, but the goal is still craft training: hear stress,
shape line lengths, place rhyme, and notice how form changes emotional motion.

## Mining checklist

After any daily exercise, extract:

- one image that could become a verse line,
- one phrase that could become a title or hook,
- one organic or kinesthetic detail,
- one surprising verb,
- one metaphor or simile candidate,
- one line to discard because it explains too soon.

Tag material by concept so later coaching can route it:

- `object-writing`,
- `metaphor`,
- `simile`,
- `title-seed`,
- `verse-image`,
- `chorus-image`,
- `point-of-view`,
- `prosody`,
- `rhyme-seed`.

## Skill workflow

When applying this file:

1. Ask whether the user wants sensory access or metaphor training if the need
   is unclear.
2. Pick one day type, not the whole curriculum.
3. Generate a specific seed or collision.
4. State the timer and the no-polish rule.
5. After the user shares writing, mine rather than rewrite immediately.
6. Route mined material to object writing, metaphor, rhyme, form, or prosody
   context only after the practice pass is complete.

## Strict timer — stop mid-word

The single most consequential discipline of *Songwriting Without Boundaries* (2011). The timer is not
guidance. The writer stops the moment the buzzer fires, including
mid-word and mid-letter.

> "Stop immediately when the timer ends." — Pat (*Songwriting Without Boundaries* (2011) challenge
> openers; paraphrase)

The discipline:

- The writer's job during the timer is access, not finishing.
- Mid-word stops train trust in the process: the page is for raw
  material, not polished output.
- Re-reading "finished thoughts" later is worse than re-reading
  fragments — fragments invite revision; finished thoughts feel
  closed.

Coaching prompt at session start: "When the buzzer sounds, stop
where you are. Do not finish the word, the sentence, or the
thought."

## Free association vs story mode

*Songwriting Without Boundaries* (2011) names two states the writer can be in during a timed write:

- **Free association** — the senses drive; the writer follows
  sensory leaps wherever they go; the seed is a doorway, not a
  contract.
- **Story mode** — the writer tells a story or describes an event
  in chronological order.

Pat permits story mode when senses pull the writer there. He warns
against forcing story mode to give the page a shape.

Diagnostic:

- If the writer is forcing a story onto the page, return to a
  specific sense (taste, touch, body sensation) to break the
  narrative grip.
- If a story arrives because the senses led there, follow it.
- Do not switch back and forth deliberately — pick whichever the
  page is already doing.

## Anti-patterns Pat names

Five patterns the timed write should reject:

1. **Explanation mode** — the page narrates what the writer is
   doing, thinking, or feeling rather than what the senses register.
2. **Polish during the write** — fixing line breaks, capitalizing,
   correcting typos while the timer runs.
3. **Rhyming during object writing** — chasing rhyme pulls the
   writer out of sense-bound access.
4. **Meter during object writing** — counting stresses pulls the
   writer into form too early.
5. **Theme statements** — naming the "point" of the write closes
   the page before the page has mined its material.

> "No judgments, comments, or quotes allowed." — Pat (*Songwriting Without Boundaries* (2011) Day 1)

## Mining patterns Pat shows in writer samples

*Songwriting Without Boundaries* (2011)'s reader-sample sections reveal three recurring critique
moves Pat applies. Use them when reviewing a writer's daily write:

1. **Spot sense clusters and their leaps.** A passage that moves
   from one sensory environment to another (elevator to ocean,
   kitchen to wartime) is doing the work; name the leap.
2. **Name the productive ambiguity.** When a line could mean two
   things and both work, mark the dual reading — do not flatten.
   See [metaphor](metaphor.md) "Productive ambiguity".
3. **Praise verbs over adjectives.** Verbs carry the writer's
   access to the body and the scene. Adjectives explain. A line
   built on surprising verbs is mined material.

Use these as critique vocabulary when the writer is ready for
feedback. Skip them during the timed write itself.

## Day 15 — the collision pairs

*Songwriting Without Boundaries* (2011), Challenge 2 opens with five adjective-noun collision pairs.
The 14-day Challenge then jumbles them across subsequent days to
test all combinations.

The five seed pairs (paraphrased shape):

| Pair | Adjective | Noun |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Lonely | Moonlight |
| 2 | Blackened | Funeral |
| 3 | Fallen | Carburetor |
| 4 | Smooth | Autumn |
| 5 | Fevered | Handkerchief |

Days 16-21 recombine the adjectives and nouns into new pairings —
for example, "Lonely Handkerchief" or "Blackened Autumn". Each new
combination becomes a fresh collision drill.

The discipline: write a coherence sentence first (one line that
makes the collision plausible), then run a 90-second sense-bound
write from the collision.

Pair-jumbling is the day-to-day mechanic — each new pairing forces
the writer to find a new linking quality. See
[metaphor](metaphor.md) "Eight named metaphor moves" for the
recipes that operate on the pairs.

## Three-stage leap — source through target

*Songwriting Without Boundaries* (2011), Challenge 3's central pattern. The metaphor is not a single
collision; it is a three-stage chain:

```text
1. Source idea       (e.g., snowstorm)
       ↓
2. Linking quality   (e.g., cold)
       ↓
3. Target idea       (e.g., your sneer)
       ↓
4. Ten-minute object write through the target lens
```

The leap matters. Most beginners skip the linking-quality stage and
jump directly from source to target — which produces strained
metaphors. Naming the quality in stage 2 is the craft move.

The reversal pattern: after writing target-through-source, turn the
chain around and write source-through-target. Reversal tests whether
the metaphor is durable.

Use reversal:

- Only when the linking quality is essential to both ideas.
- Days 32-42 of the curriculum reverse routinely.
- A reversal that feels forced means the metaphor wanted to stay
  one-directional or convert to simile.

## Stress-before-sound — why Days 43-44 forbid rhyme

*Songwriting Without Boundaries* (2011), Challenge 4 opens with no-rhyme tetrameter exercises (Days
43-44). The pedagogical reason: stress placement and rhyme are
separate skills, and learning to hear stress is harder if rhyme is
on the page distracting the ear.

Sequence:

1. **Days 43-44** — tetrameter only; no rhyme. Hear stress.
2. **Days 45-48** — tetrameter couplets; add rhyme to a hearing
   that already knows stress.
3. **Days 49-52** — common meter; mix stress patterns with rhyme.
4. **Days 53-56** — six-line shapes and abba; complex stability.

Skipping the no-rhyme days means rhyme covers for weak stress.
The remediation: any time stress feels off, drop rhyme and rewrite
the line until stress lands clean, then add rhyme back.

## Nashville stressed-vowel title brainstorming

A daily practice generator from patpattison.com seminars. Pat cites
it as the way working Nashville writers produce title candidates
quickly.

The drill:

1. Pick a stressed-vowel sound (long `a`, long `i`, short `u`,
   etc.).
2. List 20+ words sharing that vowel sound.
3. Recombine candidates into 2-3 word title phrases.
4. Pick the candidate that fits the song's emotion.
5. Test the title's rhyme surface — the same vowel must also
   support 10+ rhyme candidates.

> "This is how Nashville writers write a song every day." — Pat
> (paraphrase)

Use the Nashville method as a 10-minute daily warm-up after the
56-day curriculum. Pair with [hook](hook.md) "title generation"
for the seven-types catalog.

## Pair-jumbling mechanic (*Songwriting Without Boundaries* (2011) Days 15-16)

A subtle design detail in Challenge 2 Days 15-16 that affects how the
metaphor-collision drill works:

- **Day 15** uses 5 adjectives and 5 nouns paired one way (List 1)
- **Day 16** uses the SAME 5 adjectives and 5 nouns reshuffled into NEW
  pairings (List 2)

The pair-jumbling is deliberate. Each pair forces the writer to find a
new linking quality. By reusing the same 10 words in new combinations,
Pat tests whether the writer's linking-quality search is genuine — a
formulaic answer that worked for Day 15's pair will not work for Day
16's reshuffled pair.

Coach use: when assigning Day 15 / 16 drills, do not let the writer use
the same linking quality across days. Each pair needs its own search.

## Pat's critique-move vocabulary (*Songwriting Without Boundaries* (2011) — recurring across all four challenges)

Throughout writer-sample commentary in all four challenges, Pat applies
the same recurring diagnostic phrases. These are the coaching vocabulary
for reviewing object writes and lens writes. Treat as named moves:

| Critique move | What it directs |
|---|---|
| **Spot sense clusters and their leaps** | Identify where the writing moves from one sensory environment (kitchen) to another (highway); leaps are signals, not failures |
| **Name the productive ambiguity** | When a line could mean two things and both work, mark the dual reading explicitly |
| **Praise verbs over adjectives** | Verbs carry sensory access; adjectives explain. Underline the verbs first |
| **Invite family members** | In lens writing, count how many source-family words appear in the target-lens write — the more, the stronger the metaphor |
| **Underline the verbs** | Drill instruction for verb-strength assessment; the writer marks every verb, then evaluates whether each does work |
| **Rhyming positions are spotlights** | Content of end-line positions tells most of the story; weak words in rhyme positions are wasted spotlights |
| **Form is a road map** | Structure tells the writer where to go; rhyme shifts signal perspective shifts; line-length shifts signal pace shifts |

Use these phrases verbatim when coaching writer samples. The shorthand
makes the same critique replicable across sessions.

## Tense-flip and POV-flip as named drills (*Songwriting Without Boundaries* (2011) Challenges 1, 3)

Pat treats tense-flips and POV-flips as explicit drills, not casual
exercises. Specific instructions:

- **Tense flip** — re-read your sample in past tense, present tense, and
  future tense (or future-conditional). Compare immediacy, weight,
  vulnerability. The right tense often surfaces only by trying the
  wrong ones.
- **POV flip** — re-read your sample in 1st person, 2nd person, 3rd
  person. Note which version surprises you. The least expected POV
  often produces the strongest writing.
- **Tense-neutral -ing forms** — when a line could work across tenses,
  use the gerund or present-participle form to defer commitment. Useful
  for repeatable chorus material that needs to color differently in
  verse 1 (past) vs verse 3 (future).

The flips are revision tools, not generation tools. Run them AFTER the
object-write or lens-write is on the page; do not interrupt the timer to
flip mid-write.
