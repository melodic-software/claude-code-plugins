# Co-Writing

## Core idea

Co-writing works best when the room protects idea flow. The first job is not to
be smart, polished, or defensible. The first job is to keep material moving
until both writers hear something worth keeping.

> Silence is a request for more, more, more.

The no-free zone: no idea is argued down in the moment. If a collaborator likes
something, they say yes. If they do not like it, they stay silent and let more
ideas arrive.

**The method is not Pat's, and the Appendix is emphatic about that.** It is
Stan Webb's — Pat's first professional co-writer, who taught it to him at the
start of a Nashville session, and whom the Appendix thanks by name. Pat's
contribution is carrying it into Berklee's classrooms and adding rules 3 and 4
below — stay inside the song, and no technical talk. In the Appendix those two
arrive as a single piece of advice about not talking craft in the room; the
four-rule opener splits it in two. Attribute the method to Stan Webb when
coaching from this material; a writer told "Pat's rule" is being told something
the source takes visible care to credit elsewhere.

Two details of the original telling that carry the rule's logic:

- **The dumbest idea is load-bearing, not merely tolerated.** The chain runs
  dumb idea → less dumb idea → decent idea → great idea, and the Appendix
  reports that its own session's best part came out of its dumbest idea. The
  claim is causal: censor the first link and the last one never arrives.
- **The closed door is literal before it is a metaphor.** Webb opened the
  session by physically shutting the door and saying that nobody outside needs
  to know how dumb either writer sounds. What it buys is named precisely:
  nobody has to defend anything, so the only ideas that survive are the ones
  both writers actively love — no arguments and no compromise.

## Closed-door safety

The closed door is psychological permission. Writers need room to say awkward,
obvious, half-formed, or foolish things without performing competence.

The rule:

```text
say everything -> let weak ideas trigger better ideas -> say yes when one lands
```

One bad idea can give the other writer a less bad idea, which can lead to a
usable idea, which can lead to the line. If the bad idea is censored, the chain
never starts.

## No-Free Zone

In the no-free zone:

- Say ideas out loud.
- Do not censor for dignity.
- Do not say no.
- Use silence as "keep going."
- Say yes when something works.
- Keep only ideas both writers love.

This removes the need to defend fragile fragments. It also prevents compromise
from becoming the song's organizing principle. The final lyric is built from
shared yeses rather than negotiated tolerances.

## Inner critic

The same rule applies when writing alone. Treat the inner critic as a co-writer
who is not allowed to say no during generation.

Separate the jobs:

```text
generation: no-free zone
revision:   craft evaluation
```

The critic can return during revision with the tools in
[process](process.md), [prosody](prosody.md), [form](form.md), and
[worksheets](worksheets.md). During generation, its job is silence unless it
can say yes.

## Stay inside the song

Do not talk about writing while co-writing. Technical explanation is often a
flight from risk: explaining assonance, metaphor, rhythm, or craft can make a
fragile line sound more respectable without making it better.

Stay inside:

- The song.
- The character.
- The scene.
- The next possible line.
- The emotional situation.

If a technique is useful, use it directly in the draft. Do not pause the room
to lecture about it.

## Coaching prompts

- What idea are you withholding because it seems too obvious or foolish?
- Can you answer a weak idea with another possibility instead of critique?
- What has earned an explicit yes?
- Where did the room start talking about craft instead of writing the song?
- Which idea do both writers love enough to keep?
- Is the inner critic helping revision or interrupting generation?

## Co-writing workflow

1. Close the room psychologically: what happens in the draft stays in the
   draft.
2. Agree that silence means "more," not rejection.
3. Generate freely.
4. Say yes clearly when a word, image, title, phrase, or turn works.
5. Keep following yeses.
6. Avoid explaining technique while generating.
7. Return to craft evaluation only after the room has material.
8. Build the song from shared enthusiasm rather than compromise.

## Common fixes

- If the room freezes, restate that silence means "keep throwing ideas."
- If someone starts defending a line, return to generation and ask for more
  options.
- If the session becomes a craft lecture, ask what the character says next —
  and read it as someone protecting a line they do not trust, not as a
  discipline problem.
- If compromise appears, keep generating until both writers can say yes.
- If writing alone, park critique until the draft has enough material to revise.

## No-Free-Zone — the four rules

Pat's website condenses the room's contract into four enforceable rules
read aloud at session open. Posting them keeps the room honest when
someone slips.

1. **Say everything that comes to your head, no matter how dumb.**
   The good ideas hide inside the bad ones. Censoring the bad ones
   kills the chain.

   > "Say everything that comes to your head ... no matter how dumb
   > it is." — Pat (patpattison.com)

2. **Silence means keep going. A spoken `yes` advances the line.**
   Nobody says no. If you do not like an idea, stay silent. If you
   like one, say so out loud.

3. **Stay inside the song and the characters.**
   No meta-talk. No "is this any good", no "what are we doing", no
   "this isn't working". Speak as the song's character. If the
   character would not say it now, the character does not say it now.

4. **No technical talk while drafting.**
   No "that's family rhyme", "that's deceptive closure", "that's
   tetrameter". Technical names are revision tools; in the drafting
   room they let writers defend lines with vocabulary instead of
   making lines better.

Rules 3 and 4 are Pat's addition to Stan Webb's original two (say everything;
silence means more, `yes` advances). The Appendix gives the reason they are
needed, and it is diagnostic rather than stylistic: technical talk is usually
**fear wearing academic robes** — a writer dressing up an idea they suspect is
weak. Naming the effect does not improve the line. The Appendix's own phrasing
is that a dumb idea is still dumb with professorial robes on. When a room
starts explaining craft, treat it as a signal that someone is protecting a line
they do not trust, not as a discipline lapse to be scolded.

> "Don't be afraid to write crap — it makes the best fertilizer." — Pat
> (*Writing Better Lyrics* (2009), Appendix)

The rules are not soft suggestions. The room actually says them out
loud at the start of the session. See
[co-write-session-opener](../templates/co-write-session-opener.md) for
the printable opener.

## Session protocol

A session structure that respects the four rules:

| Stage | Time | Activity |
|---|---|---|
| Open | 2 min | Read the four rules aloud |
| Warmup | 5-10 min | Both writers object-write the same seed silently |
| Compare | 10 min | Read pages aloud; mine without critique |
| Central section | 40-60 min | Draft chorus or refrain together; trade lines |
| Outer sections | 40-60 min | Trade verses; alternate lines; parallel drafts |
| Cliche pass | 5 min per section boundary | Fresh-eyes scan; mark not fix |
| Stability check | 10 min | One pass via [stable / unstable](stable-unstable-meta.md) |
| Wrap | 10 min | Demo capture; assign solo revision; book next session |

The stages are guidelines, not gates. Skip what the room has already
done.

## Solo applications

Two of the four rules apply to solo drafting:

- Rule 1 (say everything) — write the dumb idea down; do not delete
  during the draft pass.
- Rule 4 (no technical talk) — during drafting, do not name what
  rhyme type you are using or what meter you are in. Naming during
  drafting invites defensiveness over revision.

The other two (silence-means-keep-going; yes advances the line) are
co-write-only.

## When the rules break

Common breakages and recoveries:

| Breakage | Symptom | Recovery |
|---|---|---|
| Someone says "no" or "that won't work" | Room freezes | Restate Rule 2; ask the speaker to offer an alternative instead of a rejection |
| Defensiveness about a line | One writer keeps pushing one phrasing | Apply Rule 1; write the alternative down without rejecting the original; pick later |
| Technical critique mid-draft | Room becomes a craft lecture | Apply Rule 4; ask "what does the character say next" |
| Long silence (>2 min) | Stall | Re-read last line aloud; write the next thing that comes; do not analyze |
| Compromise on a line neither writer loves | Half-hearted draft | Generate more options until both can say yes |

If two breakages happen in the same session, take a 5-minute break.
Resume by re-reading the rules.

## Title Game as session opener (Songwriter Trysts ep. 40)

Beyond the four No-Free-Zone rules, Pat documents a specific session-
opening exercise: the Title Game. Co-writers chain titles off each
other's stressed vowels for 5-10 minutes, surfacing a shared title for
the session.

The cascade defuses "my title is precious" — after 10 chained titles, no
single title feels load-bearing, and writers can let any of them go. The
exercise also reveals each writer's sonic preferences, emotional
altitudes, and instincts before drafting begins.

See [title-game](title-game.md) for full mechanics; see
[templates/title-game-prompt](../templates/title-game-prompt.md) for the
printable opener.

## Inner critic operating under No-Free Zone (solo writing)

Pat's extension to the No-Free-Zone rules: when writing solo, the same
discipline applies. The writer's inner critic becomes the second voice
in the room, and the same four rules govern the conversation between
generative-self and critic-self:

- **Rule 1** (say everything dumb) — generative-self gets to propose
  bad ideas without being shut down by critic-self
- **Rule 2** (silence = more) — when critic-self is silent on an idea,
  generative-self keeps going
- **Rule 3** (stay inside the song and the characters) — critic-self
  stays with the character and the scene rather than rising to
  commentary about the draft
- **Rule 4** (no technical talk) — critic-self does not derail into
  craft analysis during drafting; that's a separate revision pass

Most writer's block is the inner critic refusing to honor Rule 1 or
Rule 4. The discipline is the same in co-write or solo — separate
generation from revision; do not let critique kill the generative phase.

**This section and § Solo applications above disagree, and § Solo applications
is the stricter reading.** That section calls rules 2 and 3 co-write-only and
maps just rules 1 and 4 onto solo work; this one maps all four. The Appendix
supports the looser reading — it says outright that the discipline helps every
time Pat writes, not only when co-writing, and names the inner critic as his
most frequent co-writer. Treat all four as applicable solo, with the caveat
that rule 2's spoken `yes` has no literal solo equivalent beyond keeping the
line. What the Appendix does not do is rank them, so neither should coaching
built on it.
