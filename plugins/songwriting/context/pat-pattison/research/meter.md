# Meter

Pat Pattison - *Songwriting: Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure* (1991), Chapter 3.
Pat Pattison - *Writing Better Lyrics* (2009), Chapter 14-17.
Pat Pattison - *Songwriting Without Boundaries* (2011), Challenge 4.

Notation: `/` = primary stress, `//` = secondary stress, `u` = unstressed.
Pat's own source notation uses only `/` and `//`; the `u` marker is added here
as a scansion convenience and is not in Pat's text.

Chapter 3's opening epigraph sets the duple/triple movement the chapter unpacks:

> Sink like a stone that's been thrown in the ocean
> My logic has drowned in a sea of emotion
> Stop before you start
> BE STILL MY BEATING HEART
>
> — Sting, "Be Still My Beating Heart"

The first two phrases ride a long triple figure; the last two snap into duples.
The whole chapter is about hearing that contrast and using it deliberately.

## Core idea

Meter starts with syllables, then stress, then repeated stress patterns. Once a
pattern repeats enough, the listener expects it again; fulfilling, delaying, or
changing that expectation controls pace, flow, and closure.

> "To create a rhythm, simply repeat a pattern."

## Syllables

A syllable is usually one vowel sound plus consonants around it. It may be a
whole word, part of a word, or only a vowel sound.

Diphthongs contain two vowel sounds treated as one vowel. Speech may count a
diphthong as one syllable, but melody can stretch it across two notes.

Use a dictionary for word division. The writer's job is not to guess syllables
by eye; it is to hear and verify how the word actually divides.

## Conventional stress

Multi-syllable English words have conventional stress. The stressed syllable is
normally higher, louder, and longer than the syllables around it.

```text
incision:     u / u
turbulent:    / u //
understand:   // u /
relinquish:   u / u
```

If primary stress is on the first or last syllable of a three-syllable word, the
opposite end often carries secondary stress. If primary stress is in the middle,
there may be no secondary stress. Four-syllable and longer words normally carry
secondary stress.

## One-syllable words

One-syllable stress depends on function:

- Meaning carriers usually stress: nouns, main verbs, adjectives, adverbs.
- Grammar carriers usually do not: articles, prepositions, conjunctions,
  auxiliaries, personal pronouns, relative pronouns.

Grammar carriers can stress when contrast makes them important:

```text
Throw the ball TO me, not AT me.
YOU throw it to ME, not to HER.
```

Grey areas are normal, especially in strings of one-syllable words. Start with
the obvious meaning carriers, then fit weaker syllables around them.

## Scansion

Scanning means marking the stresses already present in natural speech. Do not
force a pattern onto the phrase.

Worked example:

```text
When I got home the house was dark.
u    u /   /    u   /     u   /
```

"When," "I," and "got" could change under contrast, but "home," "house," and
"dark" carry the obvious weight. Save musically strong positions for the
weight-bearing words.

## Matching patterns

Lyrics are built to fit notes. When matching a second verse to a first verse,
match both stress pattern and importance pattern.

Pattison's Sting example scans into regular groups: the first two phrases use
three-syllable patterns, and the last two use two-syllable patterns. A rewrite
is "too hot" when it crowds stressed words into unstressed positions. It is "too
cold" when weak content lands where the model had important words.

Pattern-matching checklist:

- Match stress to stress.
- Match grey areas with similar grey areas.
- Put important words in important positions.
- Keep connector words from stealing strong beats.

## Rhythm and expectation

A rhythm is a repeated stress pattern. The pattern can be small or large; size
matters less than repetition.

```text
small cell:        / u
repeated figure:   / u / u / u / u
larger cell:       / u u /
```

The more regular the repetition, the faster expectation locks in.

## Pace: duples and triples

Duple movement alternates unstressed and stressed syllables:

```text
duple:             u / u /
constant duples:   u / u / | u / u /
```

Changing from duples to triples adds unstressed syllables between stresses, so
the rhythm accelerates:

```text
duple setup:       u / u / | u / u /
triple change:     u / u / | u u / u u /
```

Changing from triples to duples removes intervening unstressed syllables, so the
rhythm decelerates:

```text
triple setup:      u u / u u /
duple change:      u / u /
```

Keep rhythmic pace separate from phrase-length speed: rhythm can slow while a
section still feels pushed forward by phrase count.

## Stress count vs syllable count

When deciding whether one phrase is longer than another for rhythmic structure,
count stressed syllables, not raw syllables. An unstressed pickup or extra weak
syllable may not change the structural weight.

If stressed syllables keep the same note length, extra stresses usually extend
the number of bars. If the stresses are squeezed into the same number of bars,
the same longer phrase can sound accelerated instead.

## Common meter

Common Meter alternates four-stress and three-stress phrases:

```text
Phrase 1: / u / u / u /   -> motion
Phrase 2: / u / u /       -> motion
Phrase 3: / u / u / u /   -> motion
Phrase 4: / u / u /       <- closure
```

It maps cleanly onto popular music's two-, four-, and eight-bar subdivisions.
With stressed syllables on strong quarter-note positions in 4/4, the four
phrases define an eight-bar unit.

Worked example: Pattison uses "Mary Had a Little Lamb" to show that stopping
after two lines feels incomplete, and that line three predicts the three-stress
line needed for line four.

## Common meter as map

In *Writing Better Lyrics* Chapter 14, common meter becomes a drafting map, not only
a scansion label. The chapter ties the 4/3/4/3 stress pattern to the eight-bar
section in Western popular music: two bars establish a resting landmark, four
bars create an unstable midpoint, and the last two bars answer the expectation
set by the matching first and third phrases.

Use common meter by counting strong stresses, not by forcing every unstressed
syllable into a fixed slot. Pattison shows the same pattern surviving extra
weak syllables, missing or softened stresses, triple-meter delivery, and lines
that divide into smaller phrases. The durable relationship is longer / shorter /
longer / shorter.

> Writing in common meter is not a goal.

That quote is the guardrail: common meter is a tool for orienting the lyricist,
not a rulebook. Draft into 4/3/4/3 to make the section's balance easy to hear;
when the content resists, check whether the resistance creates a meaningful
surprise.

Common-meter coaching checks:

- Can the lyric be heard as four stress groups: 4, 3, 4, 3?
- If line one is shortened to "3+," is it still longer than line two?
- Do extra unstressed syllables leave the strong-stress relationship intact?
- Does line three renew line one's motion strongly enough to predict closure?
- If the last line is shorter or longer than expected, does the idea deserve the
  spotlight?

## Common-meter variations

Common meter tolerates variation as long as the listener still hears relational
balance:

- Four-stress lines may become "3+" lines with a trailing unstressed syllable.
- A line may contain adjacent stresses or weak stresses without breaking the
  pattern.
- The four-stress line can split into two short phrases.
- A 4+3 pair can behave like one longer phrase.
- Triple movement can carry the same stress relationship.

Do not treat variation as sloppiness. Treat it as prosody evidence: if the
variation supports the emotional or semantic turn, keep it; if it only clouds
the map, revise.

## Unbalanced common meter

Once common meter establishes expectation, structural imbalance creates
attention. Pattison's Paul Simon examples show two useful disturbances:

- Shorten the expected last phrase to leave the section hanging when the
  emotion should feel unresolved.
- Lengthen the last phrase to turn a spotlight on the section's important idea.

The coaching rule is practical: "spotlight" positions should carry important
content. Do not turn on a spotlight for decoration.

## Common-meter spotlighting

In *Writing Better Lyrics* Chapter 15, Pattison turns common meter into a controlled
spotlight machine. Begin with a stable abab / 4-3-4-3 pattern, let the listener
expect the fourth line to rhyme with line two and carry three stresses, then
alter one variable at a time.

Core spotlight moves:

- Shorten line four to two stresses for instability and suspense.
- Lengthen line four to four stresses for deceptive rhythmic closure.
- Keep the extra line-four stress inside the line when the end rhyme still
  needs to answer line two.
- Rhyme line four with lines one and three instead of line two to make the
  closing rhyme fool the listener.
- Add one or more extra four-stress lines on the same rhyme to build pressure.
- Finally answer the delayed line-two rhyme with a three-stress line, preferably
  with a near-perfect sonic bond because the rhyme is reaching across distance.

> When you turn on spotlights, use them.

Spotlighted positions magnify weak language as much as strong language. If a
line has been structurally marked by length, rhyme surprise, or delayed payoff,
put the title, the turn, the image, or the dramatic reveal there. Do not waste
that attention on filler words.

## Deceptive vs unexpected common-meter closure

Use the listener's expectation to name the closure:

- Deceptive closure: the listener expected a specific thing and got fooled.
  In abab common meter, a fourth line that rhymes with lines one and three
  instead of line two is deceptive because the b rhyme had been predicted.
- Unexpected closure: the listener had no specific prediction, so the added
  line or sudden rhyme arrives "out of the blue." In xaxa common meter, a late
  rhyme between lines three and four surprises rather than fools.

This distinction matters for coaching. Deception depends on a clear setup; if
the setup is too loose, call the effect unexpected and strengthen the content in
the surprise position.

## Xaxa common meter

The xaxa version of common meter keeps the 4/3/4/3 stress relationship but
relaxes rhyme expectation because lines one and three do not rhyme. The same
structural moves still work:

- Add a fourth stress to line four.
- Rhyme lines three and four for a sudden closing bond.
- Extend the structure with extra four-stress lines.
- Return to the line-two rhyme at the end when the distance can be supported by
  a strong rhyme.

Because xaxa carries less rhyme pressure up front, its later rhymes tend to feel
more surprising than deceptive. Use it when a lyric needs common-meter balance
without the tighter nursery-rhyme pull of abab.

## Two-by-two meter

In *Writing Better Lyrics* Chapter 16, Pattison contrasts common meter's eight-bar
journey with four-stress couplets that organize two bars plus two bars. The
basic pattern is four stresses matched by four stresses. Because the line
lengths match, the listener can stop comfortably after line two.

> Think of line length as a traffic cop:

Matched line lengths tell the ear it may stop. Unmatched lengths create
instability and send the listener forward to find a place of rest. Rhyme
strengthens the stop sign when the matched lines rhyme as a couplet.

Two-by-two diagnostics:

- If lines one and two are both four-stress lines and rhyme, the section closes
  quickly in balanced steps.
- If the second line is three stresses, the section behaves more like common
  meter and pushes forward.
- If many four-stress lines accumulate, the section can feel stable, leisurely,
  or pressure-building depending on rhyme density.
- If a shorter unrhymed line interrupts a couplet expectation, it creates an
  IOU that can be cashed in later by rhyme, meter, or title placement.

## Couplet uses

Four-stress couplets usually rhyme and usually form a four-bar lyric and
musical unit. Use them when the song needs regular, balanced movement in short
steps.

Useful couplet moves:

- Extend a section from four lines to six for slight instability: an odd number
  of couplets can create center while still implying continuation.
- Place a couplet at the end of common meter to accelerate and create arrival.
- Begin with couplets to establish balance, then withhold the expected rhyme or
  stress length on a later line to create a longer-section IOU.
- Use several rhymed four-stress lines in a row to build pressure, then break
  the pattern with a shorter line that points forward.

When coaching a draft, separate line-length balance from rhyme balance. A line
may be metrically stable but sonically unresolved, or sonically closed while a
stress surprise keeps the section in motion.

## Mixing couplets and common meter

Mixing two-by-two couplets with common meter changes movement without requiring
new vocabulary. A verse can start in four-stress couplets, shift into common
meter for a prechorus, and return to four-stress lines in the chorus.

The useful contrast is double-edged:

- Common meter can feel faster because its shorter second and fourth lines
  arrive sooner.
- It can also feel slower because rhymes happen less frequently than in rhymed
  couplets.

That mixed effect is strong for transitions. Use it when a section must prepare
the ear for a chorus without sounding like the chorus yet.

## Structural IOUs

A structural IOU is an expectation deliberately left unpaid. Chapter 16 shows IOUs
created by:

- A three-stress line where a four-stress couplet line was expected.
- A missing rhyme in a position that had been trained to rhyme.
- A distant rhyme connection too far away to be heard unless the intervening
  structure keeps the ear waiting.

Cash an IOU only when the payoff matters. A title, emotional reversal, or
dramatic image can justify the extra attention; ordinary connective language
usually cannot.

## Managing lockstep couplets

In *Writing Better Lyrics* Chapter 17, Pattison treats repeated four-stress rhymed
couplets as useful raw material that can become deadly if every two lines stop
and start the song again. The danger pattern is aa bb cc dd ee: matched lengths
plus paired rhymes make the lyric march in small units, so every weak line
becomes more visible and the song feels longer than it is.

> Interesting structure isn't something that just happens.

The fix is to make the structure feel larger than two-line units. The more
words a section carries, the larger the structure should feel.

Couplet-expansion moves:

- Unrhyme the first couplet and leave the second couplet rhymed, converting
  a two-plus-two section into a four-line journey.
- Shorten line two to three stresses, opening the door to common-meter
  spotlight techniques.
- Insert a five-stress line after line two and another matching five-stress
  line at the end: 4/3/5/4/3/5.
- Add rhyme to lines two and five, then lines one and four, moving from loose
  xxaxxa toward tighter abcabc.
- Unrhyme a matched couplet draft first, then insert longer rhymed lines in the
  middle and at the end.
- Keep the original couplets intact but insert five-stress rhymed lines around
  them when the couplets are strong enough to survive.
- Build an eight-line structure where the fourth line withholds a rhyme and the
  eighth line answers it.

Use looseness for uncertainty, loss, or instability. Tighten rhyme and stress
organization when the lyric's emotion is factual, resolved, or controlled.

## Delayed couplet payoff

A larger couplet-based section can raise, satisfy, and then raise expectations
again. Pattison's Leonard Cohen analysis shows the key pattern: a six-line unit
establishes a shape, a partial repetition makes listeners expect the rest of
that shape, extra lines delay the expected rhyme, and the final title pays it
off under intense spotlight.

Coach delayed payoff by asking:

- What exact shape has the lyric taught the ear to expect?
- Which rhyme or line length is still missing?
- How many extra lines can the lyric sustain before the delay breaks?
- Is the final payoff stronger because the listener had to wait?
- Would plain couplets say the same ideas with less structural help?

Delayed payoff is not license to wander. Stretch only far enough for the snap
to matter.

## Structural pentad

Describe any rhythmic structure with five questions:

- Balance: symmetrical or asymmetrical?
- Pace: constant, accelerated, or decelerated?
- Flow: through-written or fragmented?
- Closure: closed or open?
- Type of closure: expected, unexpected, or deceptive?

Common Meter is symmetrical, constant, through-written, closed, and expected.

## Paradigm 1: through-written

Paradigm 1 is Common Meter. Phrase two differs from phrase one, so it does not
close internally. Phrase three repeats phrase one, making the listener expect a
return of phrase two's length at phrase four.

```text
4 stresses -> motion
3 stresses -> motion
4 stresses -> motion
3 stresses <- expected closure
```

Exercise use: write content that carries its idea through to the end, because
the structure has no earlier point of resolution.

## Paradigm 2: fragmented

Paradigm 2 repeats four matching four-stress phrases:

```text
4 stresses | 4 stresses <- internal closure
4 stresses | 4 stresses <- closure
```

Because phrase two does not differ from phrase one, the structure closes
internally after two phrases and starts over. It defines a four-bar unit as the
primary unit rather than making four bars feel like the midpoint of an
eight-bar journey.

Exercise use: divide the content into two ideas, one for each two-phrase unit.

## Deceptive closure

Deceptive closure sets up a specific ending, then closes with another phrase
length already present in the system:

```text
Expected:   4 / 3 / 4 / 3
Deceptive:  4 / 3 / 4 / 4
```

Two conditions make closure deceptive:

- The system raises an expectation for a specific resolution.
- The actual resolving phrase is already present in the structure.

The deceptive ending creates a high-profile position and unbalances the
structure. Put an important idea there.

## Unexpected closure

Unexpected closure does not fool a specific prediction. There was no specific
resolution to fool; the structure startles instead.

The common case is a system that closes and then repeats a phrase, usually the
last phrase:

```text
closed system
extra repeated phrase <- unexpected attention
```

As with deceptive closure, use the surprise position for important content.

## Common-meter exercises

- Grocery-list meter: write a grocery list in common meter twice, once in
  duple movement and once in triple movement. Keep the 4/3/4/3 stress relation
  audible even when the syllable count changes.
- Spotlight ladder: write one four-line common-meter stanza, then revise it
  through these versions: shortened fourth line, lengthened fourth line,
  fourth line rhyming with one/three, one added four-stress line, several added
  four-stress lines, and delayed return to the original line-two rhyme.
- Xaxa transfer: remove the line-one/line-three rhyme from the same stanza and
  repeat the spotlight ladder. Compare which effects feel deceptive and which
  feel unexpected.
- Couplet map: write sections for several four-stress/rhyme models: aabb,
  aaaa, aaba, abcb, abcc, ababcc, ababaccc, and abacccc. Read each aloud and
  mark where the ear stops, where it expects continuation, and where an IOU is
  created.
- Lockstep repair: take an aa bb four-stress couplet draft and transform it
  into xxaa, then 435435/xxaxxa, then 435435/abcabc. After each version, name
  whether the emotion feels looser or tighter.
- Delayed-title payoff: build a six-line unit, repeat enough of it to make the
  missing rhyme audible, delay that rhyme by one or two lines, then pay it off
  with the title.
- Common-meter contrast: rewrite one four-stress couplet verse so its middle
  section moves into common meter, then return to couplets for the title. Use
  the contrast to make the title's balance or imbalance audible.
- Long-distance rhyme repair: when the final line picks up a rhyme from several
  lines earlier, use a rhyming dictionary to find a near-perfect sound bond and
  match the final line's rhythm closely enough for the ear to accept the return.
- Everyday decision meter: write a telemarketing call, date-call hesitation, or
  similarly plain situation in common meter. The point is not literary polish;
  the point is learning to chart content into manageable stress groups.
- Detour test: draft a clean 4/3/4/3 version, then deliberately lengthen or
  shorten one phrase. Keep the detour only if it clarifies the emotional turn.

## Four-stress timed writing

For tetrameter days, count stressed syllables, not raw syllables. The line can
move in duples or triples, and it may be jagged as long as it carries four main
stresses. Do not let regularity drive the idea.

Coaching checks:

- Does each line have four clear stresses?
- Are meaning-carriers on the strongest positions?
- Are grammar carriers stealing stress only when contrast demands it?
- Is the line sense-bound rather than merely metrical?
- Does the line keep an ear open for metaphor?

Days 43-44 intentionally remove rhyme so the writer can hear stress before
chasing sound.

## Practical workflow

1. Divide words into syllables.
2. Mark conventional stress in multi-syllable words.
3. Mark meaning-carrier stress in one-syllable words.
4. Identify grey areas without forcing them.
5. Scan the first verse naturally.
6. Match stress pattern and importance pattern in later verses.
7. Decide whether the section should be through-written or fragmented.
8. Draft into common meter when the content needs a clear map.
9. Let departures from common meter earn their keep as spotlights.
10. Distinguish deceptive closure from unexpected closure by naming the
    listener's prior expectation.
11. Test whether matched four-stress lines stop as couplets or continue through
    a larger structure.
12. Mark any structural IOU and identify where it gets paid off.
13. Enlarge lockstep couplets when a section has too many two-line stops.
14. Use the structural pentad to name the effect.

## Revision moves

- Put meaning-carriers on strong positions.
- Avoid crowding important words into unstressed slots.
- Use duple-to-triple movement for acceleration.
- Use triple-to-duple movement for deceleration.
- Use Common Meter as the reference for balance and closure.
- Use common meter as a starting map, not as the goal.
- Use four-stress couplets for balanced two-by-two movement.
- Break lockstep couplets by enlarging the rhyme/stress unit.
- Use unmatched line lengths when the section should keep moving.
- Use couplet/common-meter contrast to set up a chorus title.
- Use fragmented repetition when two short ideas should sit side by side.
- Use deceptive closure only when the ending deserves the spotlight.
- Use unexpected closure when a repeated final phrase should surprise.
- Use delayed rhymes only when the sonic bond is strong enough to be heard
  across the distance.

## Structural Pentad — unified diagnostic

*Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure* (1991), Chapter 3 introduces a five-property framework Pat uses across all
four books. Every section is described by:

| # | Property | What it answers |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Balance | Even or odd count? Matched or unmatched lengths? |
| 2 | Pace | Constant, accelerating, decelerating? |
| 3 | Flow | Through-written or fragmented? |
| 4 | Closure | Closed, leans forward, stays open? |
| 5 | Type of closure | Expected, deceptive, or unexpected? |

> "The Structural Pentad." — Pat (*Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure* (1991), Chapter 3 framing)

Use the Pentad as a single diagnostic per section. Rhythm, rhyme,
phrase count, phrase length all contribute to all five. The Pentad
collapses them into one shape.

Diagnostic worksheet:

```text
Section: <verse 1 | chorus | etc.>

1. Balance:        <balanced | unbalanced — why>
2. Pace:           <constant | accelerating | decelerating>
3. Flow:           <through-written | fragmented>
4. Closure:        <closed | leans forward | open>
5. Closure type:   <expected | deceptive | unexpected>
```

The Pentad pairs with the
[Five Compositional Elements](five-compositional-elements.md). The
Elements name the levers; the Pentad names the effects those levers
produce.

## Paradigm One — Common Meter (the exemplar)

Pat's pedagogical anchor for Common Meter. 4/3/4/3 stresses,
through-written, expected closure.

```text
Paradigm One:
line 1: X X X X       (4 stresses)
line 2: X X X         (3 stresses)
line 3: X X X X       (4 stresses)
line 4: X X X         (3 stresses)

Flow:      through-written
Closure:   expected
Stability: stable
```

Use as the home base. Once a writer can write a clean Paradigm One
system, paradigms Two and Three become deliberate deviations.

## Paradigm Two — 4/4/4/4 fragmented

Same stress count on every line; flow fragmented (often heard as 2 + 2);
expected closure.

```text
Paradigm Two:
line 1: X X X X       (4 stresses)
line 2: X X X X       (4 stresses)
line 3: X X X X       (4 stresses)
line 4: X X X X       (4 stresses)

Flow:      fragmented
Closure:   expected
Stability: stable but blocky
```

Useful when the section wants to feel even, declarative, or chant-like.
Avoid when through-written motion is needed.

## Paradigm Three — 4/3/4/4 deceptive closure

Through-written like Paradigm One, but the fourth line breaks the
expected 3-stress closure by extending to 4 stresses. Section sounds
like closing on schedule, then refuses.

```text
Paradigm Three:
line 1: X X X X       (4 stresses)
line 2: X X X         (3 stresses)
line 3: X X X X       (4 stresses)
line 4: X X X X       (4 stresses — deceptive)

Flow:      through-written
Closure:   deceptive
Stability: unstable at the closure point
```

Use Paradigm Three when the last idea must spotlight or unsettle. The
expectation of a 3-stress close makes the longer line audible.

## Paradigm comparison — One vs Two

Teaching move: write the same idea in Paradigm One and Paradigm Two.
The words feel different because the flow shape differs.

| Aspect | Paradigm One | Paradigm Two |
|---|---|---|
| Stresses | 4/3/4/3 | 4/4/4/4 |
| Flow | through-written | fragmented |
| Length perception | Long arc | Two paired chunks |
| Default mood | Expansive, narrative | Even, declarative |
| Best for | Verses that develop | Choruses that declare |

When unsure, draft both and sing each. The ear picks faster than
the page.

## Duple-to-triple and triple-to-duple physics

Mechanical explanation of pace change:

- Duple to triple **adds** unstressed syllables between stresses.
  Line accelerates: more syllables fit in the same beat count.
- Triple to duple **removes** unstressed syllables. Line decelerates:
  fewer syllables per beat.

```text
Duple line:    X . X . X . X .         (8 syllables, 4 stresses)
Triple line:   X . . X . . X . . X . . (12 syllables, 4 stresses)
```

Same 4-stress count. Different syllable counts. Different speeds.

Use in revision:

- A line that feels slow without changing meaning may want triple.
- A line that feels rushed may want duple.
- A line that switches pace mid-section needs a duple-to-triple
  transition (or vice versa).

## Goldilocks pattern matching (*Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure* (1991), Chapter 3)

Pat's memorable framing for stress pattern matching is a three-state
diagnostic: too hot, too cold, just right.

- **Too hot** — a stressed syllable in a slot that needs to be unstressed.
  The line pushes harder than it should at that beat. The ear trips.
- **Too cold** — an unstressed syllable where the structure wants stress.
  The line loses energy in a hot spot.
- **Just right** — content word stress + structural strong-beat aligned.
  The natural reading and the meter agree.

Use the Goldilocks frame when running pattern-match audits per line.
Greedy spots (per `prosody.md`) are usually too-hot or too-cold failures.

## Pitch-based stress model (*Songwriting Without Boundaries* (2011), Challenge 4)

Pat's distinctive Challenge 4 teaching frame: English stressed syllables are
not only louder and longer than unstressed — they're also **higher in
pitch**. Treating stress as pitch makes the underlying music of speech
audible.

The model:

- **Tonic (do)** — unstressed syllable pitch
- **Sol** (a fifth above) — primary stress
- **Fa** (a fourth above) — typical strong-stress
- **Mi** (a third above) — secondary stress

<!-- phonetic stress spelling trips the spell-checker --><!-- spellchecker:off -->
So a content word like "lonely" carries `sol-do` (loo-uh-LEE-NLY... actually
`SOL-do`); a two-syllable preposition like "before" carries `mi-do` —
secondary stress, not full primary. This matters when setting melody: pitch
contours that mirror natural speech stress feel inevitable; pitch contours
that fight it feel forced.
<!-- spellchecker:on -->

### Compound word stress rule

<!-- stress-caps compound examples trip the spell-checker --><!-- spellchecker:off -->
Primary stress on **compound words** in English almost always lands on the
**first syllable**. Examples: HOMEwork, RAINbow, FOOTball, SUITcase. The
second syllable carries secondary stress, not primary.
<!-- spellchecker:on -->

This rule conflicts with default phrase-stress reading; it must be applied
word-by-word.

### Two-syllable prepositions — secondary stress

Two-syllable prepositions (`óver`, `befóre`, `accróss`, `aróund`, `intó`)
carry secondary stress on one syllable — but the pitch rise is `mi` (third
above tonic), not `sol`/`fa` (fourth/fifth). This is internal stress, not
full content-word stress.

### The "into" rule (*Songwriting Without Boundaries* (2011), Challenge 4)

Pat: "into" is probably the most badly handled word in songwriting.

The conventional reading is **ínto** (stressed on first syllable). When
writers set it as **intó** (stressed on second syllable, to fit a melody
that needs the stress on "to"), the ear knows it's wrong even if the
listener can't name what's wrong.

Fix: rephrase the line so "into" sits in its natural ínto reading, OR
substitute another preposition that fits the stress pattern.

## "In Memoriam" quatrain (*Songwriting Without Boundaries* (2011), Challenge 4)

A named variant of the abba quatrain, sourced from Tennyson's *In Memoriam
A.H.H.*: four equal-length tetrameter lines, abba rhyme scheme.

Structure:

```text
4-stress line  a
4-stress line  b
4-stress line  b
4-stress line  a
```

The equal line lengths + the abba pattern (rhyme returning to the
outermost position after a paired inner rhyme) create suspended-emotion
texture. The unresolved-then-resolved arc is appropriate for mourning,
loss, retrospective, dream-state.

Pat lists this form in the Challenge 4 / 14-day rhythm-and-rhyme
curriculum. Distinct from the looser `abba` Pat uses elsewhere — In
Memoriam constrains line length to equal tetrameter, where general `abba`
allows mixed lengths.

## Pentad cross-domain applicability (*Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure* (1991), Chapter 3)

Pat's Structural Pentad (number of lines / length of lines / rhythm /
rhyme scheme / rhyme type) applies not only to rhyme structure. It also
applies to:

- **Rhythmic structure** — same pentad properties measured against the
  song's rhythmic motifs
- **Melodic structure** — same properties measured against melodic phrases
  (when melody is known)
- **Harmonic structure** — chord pattern stability per pentad property

This is the principle that lets the same diagnostic work across lyric,
melody, and harmony in `stable-unstable-meta.md`. The pentad is one
analysis frame applied across multiple structural surfaces.
