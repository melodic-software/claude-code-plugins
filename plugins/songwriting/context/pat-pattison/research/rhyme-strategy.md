## Core idea

Choose rhyme schemes by the job the section must do. Rhyme can outline idea
movement, support meaning through prosody, and create relationships between
sections.

> "Prosody is one of the most important strategies you have"

This file focuses on decision-making. For definitions and mechanics, see
[rhyme fundamentals](rhyme-fundamentals.md).

## The four control questions

Before changing words, diagnose what the rhyme scheme is doing:

- Scheme: which sounds repeat, which lines are unrhymed, and where identities
  are masquerading as rhymes?
- Structure: does the scheme balance, unbalance, fragment, or through-write the
  section?
- Pace: are rhymes close enough to accelerate, far enough apart to decelerate,
  or regular enough to stay constant?
- Closure: does the section close expectedly, deceptively, unexpectedly, or
  remain open?

These questions turn rhyme into a craft lever instead of a word-hunt.

## Strategy 1: outline idea movement

Use rhyme structure to draw the shape of the thought. Check the result against
[prosody](prosody.md) when the rhyme pattern changes the section's emotional
motion.

Fragmented schemes section off ideas:

```text
a a b b

Idea 1: lines 1-2
Idea 2: lines 3-4
```

Through-written schemes carry the thought forward:

```text
a b a b

Idea keeps moving through all four lines
```

Worked contrast from Chapter 4:

```text
aabb: Love her or leave her to me
      Keep her or let her go free
      Don't go two-timing her
      'less you're resignin' her

abab: Some girls like their flirtin'
      They're always on the roam
      Blind to who they're hurtin'
      Their eyes are never home
```

The first naturally divides into two parts. The second stays in motion because
the first rhyme waits across intervening material before resolving.

## Through-write when the idea should flow

Use through-written rhyme when a lyric idea should feel continuous, cumulative,
or unresolved until the end.

Good uses:

- A sentence that unfolds across the whole section.
- A narrative action that should not stop early.
- A verse that should lean toward a later chorus.
- A thought that should feel seamless rather than boxed into couplets.

Revision move: if a section feels chopped into little paragraphs, replace
nearby couplet closure with a scheme that delays resolution.

## Fragment when the idea should divide

Use fragmented rhyme when the content naturally has two or more units.

Good uses:

- Claim plus response.
- Problem plus consequence.
- Image plus interpretation.
- Setup plus turn.

Fragmentation is not a flaw. It is wrong only when the content wants motion and
the rhyme keeps applying the brakes.

## Strategy 2: support meaning through prosody

The rhyme scheme should behave like the emotional or dramatic action.

For a leisurely verse, spread rhymes apart. For a chorus that bursts forward,
move to consecutive rhymes. Pattison's "Ready or Not" example uses a more
leisurely verse pattern and a fast consecutive chorus pattern so the rhyme pace
matches the content's change in energy.

```text
verse:  spread rhymes -> room, thought, pressure building
chorus: d d d d       -> immediate launch, compact hook energy
```

Prosody can also operate inside one section. If the idea becomes more active or
pressurized, accelerate the rhyme scheme near the place where the lyric becomes
more active.

## Accelerate strategically

Acceleration comes from bringing rhymes closer together.

```text
a
b
c
c
c
b
```

In Chapter 4's examples, the consecutive `c c c` cluster increases pressure before a
later return. Feminine rhymes can heighten comedy because the two-syllable tail
adds bounce while the cluster speeds up.

Use acceleration for:

- Rising attraction.
- Panic or comic escalation.
- Physical action.
- A thought narrowing toward obsession.
- A title or hook that needs pressure behind it.

Do not accelerate just because the rhyme words are available. If the next verse
must reuse the scheme, the next verse needs an idea that can carry the same
late pressure.

## Decelerate strategically

Deceleration comes from moving rhymes farther apart or returning from
consecutive rhyme to alternating rhyme.

Use deceleration for:

- Reflection.
- Regret.
- Explanatory or narrative space.
- A section that should relax before contrast.
- A bridge idea that should not inherit a high-pressure verse scheme.

If a repeated section has one verse about excitement and another about calm,
the same rhyme acceleration may betray the calmer content. Solve by changing
the scheme, changing the second idea, or moving the calmer idea to a bridge.

## Closure as spotlight

Closure positions attract attention. Expected closure feels satisfying.
Deceptive and unexpected closure turn the lights on harder.

Deceptive closure is useful when a line should surprise but still feel
structurally earned:

```text
expected:   a b a b
deceptive:  a b a a
```

Unexpected closure is useful when a line should arrive from outside the
listener's prediction or when a closed system adds one more phrase.

Worked example: "SHE'LL PUT YOU ON THE ROPES" uses deceptive closure at phrase
four and then an unexpected extra title phrase at phrase five. The title lands
in the closing spotlight.

## Strategy 3: relate sections

Use rhyme to decide how sections speak to each other.

Ask:

- Should the verse move forward or stop?
- Should the chorus answer with stronger closure?
- Should the bridge relax, interrupt, or reframe the rhyme behavior?
- How strong should the contrast be between sections?

If two sections use different rhyme strategies, make the difference audible and
meaningful. A through-written verse into a fragmented chorus can feel like
motion resolving into a hook. A fragmented verse into a through-written chorus
can feel like the hook opens the song outward.

## Working from idea to scheme

Most of the time, start with the idea:

1. Name the emotional or dramatic motion.
2. Decide whether it should stop or continue.
3. Pick through-written or fragmented flow.
4. Choose rhyme spacing for pace.
5. Decide the closure type.
6. Draft rhyme words that support the movement.

Example decision:

```text
idea: a crush becomes insomnia
motion: pressure rises, then cannot sleep
scheme: a b c c c b
effect: the c cluster accelerates the middle; final b closes the frame
```

## Working from scheme to idea

Repeated song sections often force the reverse process. Verse 1 establishes a
scheme; verse 2 must find content that fits it.

Chapter 4's exercise uses `ABACCB`:

```text
scheme: ABACCB
plot: lovers dancing, spouse enters, discovery
words: dance / embrace / romance / eyes / surprise / disgrace
```

The method:

1. Mark where the scheme opens, accelerates, fragments, and closes.
2. Sketch a plot whose action fits that motion.
3. Pick rhyme words after the plot has a shape.
4. Adjust phrase length and rhythm to follow the same structural motion.

This prevents second verses from becoming content stuffed into a shape that no
longer fits.

## Exercise patterns worth keeping

Use these as coaching prompts:

- Rewrite an `aabb` passage into `abab` while preserving as much meaning as
  possible; notice how the idea must become more continuous.
- Rewrite an `abab` passage into `aabb`; notice how the idea wants two smaller
  units.
- Given a scheme such as `AABBCC`, `XAXABB`, or `AABCCC`, invent a plot whose
  action fits the scheme before choosing rhyme words.
- For a repeated verse scheme, test whether the next verse's idea can carry the
  same pressure curve.

## Diagnostic workflow

For any draft:

1. Label the rhyme scheme.
2. Mark identities separately from rhymes.
3. Circle consecutive rhymes.
4. Mark internal closures.
5. Label the system through-written or fragmented.
6. Label the ending expected, deceptive, unexpected, open, or ambiguous.
7. Compare the rhyme motion to the lyric's idea motion.
8. Revise the scheme before forcing better words into a wrong structure.

## Revision moves

- If ideas feel boxed in, through-write the scheme.
- If ideas feel shapeless, fragment the scheme into smaller balanced units.
- If energy needs to rise, move rhymes closer together.
- If energy needs to relax, spread rhymes apart.
- If an important line is being missed, move it into a closure position.
- If a title needs extra attention, consider deceptive or unexpected closure.
- If a later verse fights the scheme, change the scheme, change the verse idea,
  or move the different idea to a bridge.

## Rhyme types as strategy

*Essential Guide to Rhyming* (2014), Chapter 9 adds a second decision layer: after choosing the rhyme scheme, choose
the rhyme type that gives each structural position the right amount of push,
landing, and emotional stability.

Rhyme schemes create expected motion. Rhyme types can reinforce, soften, or
destabilize that motion.

> Isolate, understand, compose.

Use [rhyme types](rhyme-types.md) for definitions. Use this section when the
question is strategic: which type belongs in which position and why?

## Dominant and tonic positions

In an `abab` scheme, the first three rhyme positions create expectation:

```text
a
b
a  -> dominant push toward the expected b
b  -> tonic resolution
```

The third line, the second `a`, acts like a dominant position because it asks
the listener to expect the second `b`. The fourth line, the second `b`, acts
like a tonic position because it resolves the expectation.

The same idea can be reversed by swapping which sound family occupies `a` and
`b`. What matters is the structural job:

- Dominant: push, lean, heighten expectation.
- Tonic: land, resolve, close, or refuse to fully close.

Skill behavior: when analyzing a repeated section, mark not only the rhyme
scheme but also each important rhyme's structural position.

## Stability scale for strategic use

The practical scale from most stable to least stable:

```text
perfect -> family -> additive/subtractive -> assonance -> consonance
```

Strategic reading:

- Perfect rhyme gives the strongest push in dominant position and the strongest
  resolution in tonic position.
- Family rhyme works as a near-perfect substitute: most of the force, less
  slam.
- Additive/subtractive rhyme keeps much of the vowel identity while opening the
  connection slightly.
- Assonance rhyme weakens the push or landing because only the vowel connects.
- Consonance rhyme is still a connection, but it leaves the gate wide open
  because the vowel has changed.

This is why the same `abab` scheme can feel stable, light, dreamy, unstable, or
longing depending on the rhyme types placed in the dominant and tonic slots.

## Perfect rhyme in stable schemes

Perfect rhyme maximizes motion. In an `abab` scheme it creates a hard dominant
push and a hard tonic arrival. In an `aabb` scheme it strongly separates the two
couplets by closing the first pair before the second pair begins.

Use perfect rhyme when:

- the line needs arrival,
- the emotion is settled or declared,
- the chorus should feel home-like,
- a repeated hook should slam shut,
- a section needs unmistakable closure.

Watchout: repeated perfect couplets can make a song feel long because the
listener must stop and restart every two lines.

## Family rhyme for softer stability

Family rhyme keeps the same stressed vowel and uses related post-vowel
consonants. Strategically, it is stable enough to behave like rhyme but softer
than perfect rhyme.

In dominant position:

- it pushes, but not as hard as perfect rhyme,
- it lowers intensity,
- it can imply hesitation or gentleness.

In tonic position:

- it lands, but opens the gate slightly,
- it can keep the next idea available,
- it can make a resolution feel less final.

Use family rhyme when the structure should still be legible but the emotion
should not be fully locked.

## Assonance for drift

Assonance in dominant position produces little forward push unless another
element supplies pressure, such as common meter, short line length, melodic
lift, or syntax.

Assonance in tonic position is more destabilizing. A hard dominant push can land
on a soft vowel-only connection and leak energy instead of closing.

Use assonance when:

- the lyric needs drift, dream, mist, longing, or looseness,
- the section should keep moving after the expected landing,
- perfect rhyme would overstate certainty,
- the word meaning itself benefits from a less closed sound.

Do not use assonance merely to avoid a hard rhyme. Use it when a softer landing
supports the emotional state.

## Consonance for open longing

Consonance changes the vowel while keeping a final consonant or consonant
cluster. In song, where vowels carry, this is a remote connection.

In dominant position:

- it barely nudges forward,
- it gives more pressure than no rhyme,
- it may need meter or syntax to carry the expectation.

In tonic position:

- it opens the gate,
- it can create a felt pull back toward the expected sound,
- it can suggest sadness, reluctance, instability, or unresolved longing.

Important distinction: an unrhymed fourth line may feel disconnected; a
consonance rhyme in the fourth line can feel connected-but-unresolved.

## Decision matrix

Use this matrix after labeling the scheme:

| Section need | Dominant position | Tonic position |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strong declaration | Perfect | Perfect |
| Stable but not slammed | Family | Perfect or family |
| Gentle lean into closure | Family | Family |
| Dreamy or floating | Assonance | Family or assonance |
| Longing, uncertainty | Family or consonance | Consonance |
| Almost no closure | Assonance or consonance | Assonance or consonance |
| Contrast inside repeated sections | Weaker type in unstable section | Stronger type in stable section |

If the section already has strong meter, syntax, melody, or line-length
pressure, the rhyme type can be weaker and still read as connected. If the
section has little other pressure, weak rhyme types may feel like no structure.

## Repeated-section problem

Repeated song sections often require the same line lengths and melodic
structure while the emotional content changes. That creates a strategic problem:
one verse may need instability, while a later verse or chorus needs stability.

Do not assume the solution is changing the rhyme scheme. If the melody and
section architecture must match, keep the scheme but change rhyme types.

Workflow:

1. Map the repeated section's rhyme positions.
2. Label the emotional state of each appearance.
3. Keep scheme and meter where the song requires repetition.
4. Use less stable rhyme types in the unstable appearance.
5. Use more stable rhyme types in the resolved appearance.
6. Check whether the song's larger journey now grows rather than repeats.

This is the strategic point of the Randy Newman case study in Chapter 9: a remote
family/consonance connection can keep an early prechorus unstable, while a
perfect rhyme in the matching later prechorus can close firmly. The same formal
slot changes emotional color through rhyme type alone.

## Rhyme type placement workflow

When revising a draft:

1. Label the scheme.
2. Mark dominant and tonic positions.
3. Label each end rhyme by type using [rhyme types](rhyme-types.md).
4. Compare the type's stability to the line's emotional job.
5. If the line closes too hard, move down the stability scale.
6. If the line feels underpowered, move up the stability scale.
7. If repeated sections need different emotional color, keep the scheme and
   adjust type.
8. Check with [rhyme sonic bonding](rhyme-sonic-bonding.md) if interior sounds
   are carrying or fighting the end-rhyme motion.

## Chapter 9 exercises as coaching prompts

Use these as practice modes:

- Exercise 9.1: Write common meter in `abab` using only perfect rhyme; comment
  on whether the stable structure supports the idea.
- Exercise 9.2: Write `abab` twice, first with family rhyme in the dominant
  position and perfect rhyme in the tonic position, then reversed; compare the
  emotional force.
- Exercise 9.3: Repeat the test with assonance rhyme in dominant versus tonic
  position.
- Exercise 9.4: Combine assonance and family rhyme in opposite positions and
  listen for how widely the gate opens.
- Exercise 9.5: Use consonance with family rhyme in opposite dominant/tonic
  positions; compare connection-without-closure against no rhyme.

Skill prompt:

```text
Analyze this section's rhyme strategy.
Label the rhyme scheme, dominant positions, tonic positions, and rhyme types.
Then say whether each rhyme type supports the section's emotional stability.
Suggest one revision that moves up the stability scale and one that moves down.
```

## Challenge 4 rhyme practice

Challenge 4 in *Songwriting Without Boundaries* turns rhyme strategy into timed
writing practice. The writer begins with unrhymed four-stress lines, then adds
couplets, common meter, six-line forms, and unstable `abba` structures.

> Form is a road map.

Use the forms as movement choices, not as decorative containers:

| Form | Strategic effect |
| --- | --- |
| Tetrameter couplets | Strong two-line stops; useful for drive or march. |
| Common meter `xaxa` | Four-line unit with relaxed closure on trimeter lines. |
| Common meter `abab` | Tighter four-line unit; stronger expectation and return. |
| Six-line `aabccb` | Couplet energy plus pentameter push and delayed closure. |
| Six-line `xxaxxa` | Line-length motion with very delayed rhyme arrival. |
| Six-line `abcabc` | Through-written motion with late returns. |
| Six-line `xabxab` | Line-length push with one inner rhyme family. |
| `abba` / `axxa` | Floating instability; useful for loss, hope, and dream. |

Challenge 4 repeatedly shows that line length can create stronger road signs
than rhyme. Rhyme can either cooperate with that motion or confuse it. When
line length says "move forward" but rhyme closes hard, decide which signal the
song actually needs.

Coaching checks:

- Does the rhyme scheme fragment the idea too soon?
- Would a delayed rhyme let the thought travel farther?
- Are rhyme positions carrying story, image, or title-worthy language?
- Would a weaker rhyme type support instability better than perfect rhyme?
- Does the scheme's stability match the emotional camp of the lyric?
- Are secondary-stress rhymes placed where the melody can honor their natural
  stress?

Practice prompt:

```text
Choose one timed seed and one form:
- tetrameter couplets,
- common meter xaxa,
- common meter abab,
- six-line aabccb,
- six-line xxaxxa,
- six-line abcabc,
- six-line xabxab,
- abba or axxa.

Write for the timer. Then label the rhyme scheme, rhyme types, closure points,
and any line-length pressure. Revise one line so the rhyme strategy better
matches the emotional motion.
```

## The three rhyme strategies (named)

*Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure* (1991), Chapter 4 names three rhyme strategies. The skill applies each
separately or in combination per section.

### Strategy 1 — Outline how ideas move

Rhyme scheme as map of idea movement. Different schemes fragment or
join ideas across the section.

| Scheme | Effect on idea movement |
|---|---|
| `aabb` | Fragments each pair; each couplet closes a thought |
| `abab` | Through-writes; idea spans the four-line arc |
| `abba` | Encloses; outer pair frames the inner pair |
| `xaxa` | Through-writes with relaxed structure |
| `aaba` | Points forward toward a fourth `b` that does not arrive |
| `abcb` | Closes the rhymed pair; leaves first and third open |

Use Strategy 1 to decide which idea-shape the section wants:
fragmented declarations, single arcs, framed center, or unresolved push.

### Strategy 2 — Support meaning (prosody)

Pick rhyme density to match the section's emotional pace. Close
rhymes accelerate; spaced rhymes decelerate; missing rhymes float.

| Pace need | Rhyme density |
|---|---|
| Contemplative verse | Spaced (every other line, or every 4) |
| Active chorus | Close (couplets, internal rhymes) |
| Drifting prechorus | Missing or weak (no rhyme on most lines) |
| Settled refrain | Strong final pair |

Strategy 2 is what Pat calls the accelerator-pedal use of rhyme:
the closer the rhymes, the faster the lyric.

### Strategy 3 — Relate sections

Rhyme in one section sets up contrast or echo in the next. A verse
with sparse rhyme makes a chorus with dense rhyme feel arrival.
A chorus that echoes the verse's scheme creates continuity.

| Relationship | Verse scheme | Chorus scheme |
|---|---|---|
| Strong contrast | abab | aabbcc (couplet flood) |
| Subtle contrast | xaxa | abab |
| Continuity | abab | abab (with stronger rhyme types) |
| Disruption | abab | xxxx (no rhyme; spoken-word feel) |

Strategy 3 is the section-to-section move. It governs how the song's
rhyme architecture composes, not just how a single section behaves.

## Decision matrix — which rhyme type when

*Essential Guide to Rhyming* (2014), Chapter 9 synthesizes a decision matrix for rhyme type by
emotional intent. Pat's stance: rhyme type is not a default, it is
a choice per slot.

| Emotional intent | Rhyme type |
|---|---|
| Confident arrival, declarative closure | Perfect (fully resolved) |
| Resolved but fresh; cliche-avoidance | Family |
| Open-vowel start; cliche-avoidance via consonant addition | Additive |
| Cluster-ending; declutter via consonant removal | Subtractive |
| Connection without closure; held vowel carrying | Assonance |
| Shape of resolution without certainty; consonant carrying | Consonance |
| Feminine word but no full close | Partial |
| Connection only at unstressed final syllable | Weak-syllable |
| Spotlight without overclose | Deceptive closure (any type) |
| Surprise after expected close | Unexpected closure |

The matrix is read top-down per slot:

1. Name the emotional intent at the slot.
2. Identify the candidate rhyme type from the matrix.
3. Run the worksheet for that type.
4. If candidates fail the line, drop to the next less-stable type.
5. Sing the result.

## Shelley anchor — through-written without internal fragmentation

*Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure* (1991), Chapter 4 uses Shelley's "Ozymandias" as a teaching anchor (the
poem itself is public domain). The sonnet's rhyme scheme deliberately
avoids `aabb` adjacency. Each pair is separated, so no internal
couplet closes early and fragments the sonnet's single argument.

The lesson: rhyme adjacency forces structural decisions even before
the words are chosen. A long single thought benefits from spread
rhymes; a section made of short paired thoughts benefits from
couplet adjacency.

Use the Shelley principle when:

- a verse needs to read as one continuous thought,
- a section's argument depends on the listener following from line
  1 to line N without internal stops,
- the writer is tempted to use couplets out of habit rather than
  intent.

> "Nothing can match rhyme's power" to control flow. — Pat (*Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure* (1991), Chapter 4)

## Sound vs order — the two forces

*Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure* (1991), Chapter 4 separates two distinct forces that drive rhyme's effect
in a section:

- **Sound repetition** — the ear's desire for the same sound to come
  back. Strong rhyme satisfies sound repetition.
- **Order repetition** — the ear's desire for the same pattern to
  come back. A scheme like `abab` repeating across stanzas
  satisfies order repetition.

The two forces work independently. A section can satisfy sound
repetition without order repetition (irregular scheme but strong
rhymes) or vice versa (regular scheme but weak rhymes).

Diagnostic:

- If a section feels random in sound but orderly on the page, sound
  repetition is weak; strengthen rhyme types.
- If a section feels chaotic in shape but sonically rich, order
  repetition is weak; tighten the scheme.

Most sections want both forces aligned. Mismatches are tools, not
defaults.

## Paul Simon "50 Ways to Leave Your Lover" — strategic consonance (*Essential Guide to Rhyming* (2014), Chapter 6)

A named example for using consonance rhyme to relax verse motion
strategically — not by accident.

Pat cites Verse 3 of Paul Simon's "50 Ways to Leave Your Lover" as
demonstrating consonance rhyme as a deceleration tool:

- The verse's three rhyme-position words share consonant endings (pain /
  again / explain) — all -ane ending consonance, technically a partial
  rhyme since the stressed vowels differ
- The consonance creates structural continuation without the harder
  closure of perfect rhyme
- The verse keeps the song "lighter" than the verses that use perfect rhyme
- The placement is intentional — verse 3 is meant to feel less weighted
  than verses 1-2, and the rhyme stability shift carries that intent

### What this teaches

Choose rhyme stability by the section's emotional intent, not by what
came up first. A perfect rhyme can over-close a verse that wants to keep
moving. A consonance rhyme can under-close a chorus that needs to land.
Match stability to weight; pick deliberately.
