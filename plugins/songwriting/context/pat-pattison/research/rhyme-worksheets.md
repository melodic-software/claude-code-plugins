## Core idea

Make the rhyme search before drafting lines. A worksheet puts the writer in
known terrain, so phrase-end positions can be chosen for meaning rather than
panic-filled availability.

The analogy is practical: if you want good friends, look where compatible
people are likely to be. If you want useful rhymes, look from inside the song's
idea before you write yourself into a corner.

## What a worksheet is for

A rhyme worksheet is not a finished lyric, a rhyme dump, or a display of clever
language. It is a map of possible spotlight words and idea-connected rhyme
families.

It helps the writer:

- Find words that fit the lyric idea before line endings are locked.
- Avoid choosing target words with weak rhyme potential.
- Discover new story, metaphor, or emotional directions from rhyme families.
- Keep cliches, identities, and awkward grammar out of important positions.
- Enter drafting with more than one route through the lyric.

Skill behavior: when a user asks for rhymes, first decide whether they need a
single rhyme answer or a worksheet. If they are drafting a song section, a
worksheet is usually better.

## Step 1: focus the idea

Start by asking what the lyric says and what it could say. The answer can be a
rough prose sketch. It does not need rhyme, meter, line breaks, or beautiful
language yet.

Useful focus questions:

- Who is speaking?
- Who is being addressed?
- What changed?
- What pressure is the speaker under?
- What image, setting, or relationship keeps recurring?
- What does the speaker want?
- What is the emotional turn of the section?
- What could the title mean beyond its first obvious meaning?

The example chapter starts from a simple contrast between risky attraction and
the desire for safety, then expands it into a relational situation: attraction
to a socially magnetic person creates insecurity and exclusion.

Skill behavior: if the user's idea is vague, ask for or generate a one-paragraph
idea sketch before producing rhyme families.

## Step 2: make a central word list

Pull words from the idea sketch. Add title words, emotional state words,
actions, relationship terms, images, and pressure words. Put them in a numbered
central list that can be referenced while searching.

The source worksheet uses mostly masculine search targets and deliberately
chooses words with different vowel sounds. That widens the map. If all seed
words share the same vowel family, the worksheet collapses into one narrow
sound area.

Seed-word categories:

- Title words.
- Emotional words.
- Action words.
- Relationship words.
- Conflict words.
- Concrete images.
- Words that imply the section's turn.
- Words with promising vowel variety.

Do not treat the seed list as final. It is only a list of words to test.

## Include title words

Exercise 3.2 makes the title part of the worksheet habit. If a title contains
several important words, put each important word into the central list before
adding other material.

Why:

- Title words are likely to become spotlight words.
- They may need to be protected from weak rhyme positions.
- Their rhyme families can suggest better title placement.
- A title word may have poor rhyme potential, warning the writer not to force
  it into an end rhyme.

Skill behavior: when a user gives a title, split it into important words and
test each one as a seed.

## Step 3: search actively

Look up each seed word, but write down only rhyme candidates that fit the lyric
idea. This is the difference between a worksheet and a raw dictionary copy.

Active search means:

- Add a better seed word if the search reveals one.
- Remove a seed word that has poor promise.
- Change tense, part of speech, or wording if a related form generates better
  options.
- Skip words that are grammatically awkward in phrase-end position.
- Skip obvious cliches unless the song can renew them.
- Mark identities rather than accidentally treating them as rhymes.
- Follow cross-references in the dictionary instead of stopping too early.

The writer is allowed to adjust the basic approach during the search. The
worksheet is a thinking tool, not a compliance form.

## Filtering candidates

After the raw search, filter the list by function. Reject or mark:

- Transitive verbs that need an object and therefore land awkwardly at the end
  of a line.
- Cliche pairs that make the listener predict the next word.
- Identities that repeat too much of the target sound.
- Words that technically rhyme but do not fit the song's relationship,
  setting, or tone.
- Words that produce comic energy in a serious section.
- Words whose grammar would force the line into an unnatural syntax.

Keep:

- Words that fit the idea.
- Words that deepen the emotional situation.
- Words that open a new but related story path.
- Words with useful stress and phrase-end behavior.
- Words that can carry spotlight attention.

## Change the seed if needed

One important move in the chapter is changing a seed word to a nearby form
because the new form generates more useful rhymes. This is not cheating. It is
craft.

Examples of acceptable changes:

- Change tense.
- Change noun to verb or verb to noun.
- Replace an adjective with a noun from the same field.
- Replace a weak title-adjacent word with a stronger synonym.
- Move a poor-rhyming word out of the rhyme position and let a related word
  carry the end.

Skill behavior: when the target word has poor rhyme potential, offer a
"nearby seed" option rather than only giving weak rhymes.

## When a seed should be dropped

Some words should not be put into rhyme positions even if they matter to the
song. A word can be central to the idea and still be a poor end-rhyme target.

Drop or protect a seed when:

- It yields too few useful rhymes.
- Its best rhymes are off-tone.
- Its rhymes are all cliches.
- Its sound family forces the section toward comedy.
- It tempts the writer toward awkward grammar.

The word can still appear inside a line, in a title, or in an unrhymed position.
Do not confuse importance with end-rhyme suitability.

## Charted territory

The point of the preliminary work is to make drafting less blind. The worksheet
does take time, but it also saves time because the writer is not solving rhyme,
idea, grammar, and line shape simultaneously at every phrase ending.

Skill coaching frame:

```text
No worksheet: line ending chosen -> panic search -> forced rhyme -> weak idea
Worksheet:    idea map -> candidate families -> chosen spotlight -> draft line
```

The second route is slower at first and faster in revision.

## Exercise 3.1: matching rhymes and ideas

Exercise pattern to preserve:

- Start with a focused idea sketch.
- Build a seed list from the sketch.
- Use a rhyming dictionary for each seed.
- Copy only candidates that fit the idea.
- Leave room around each seed for candidate groups.

The source example uses fear, attention, exclusion, risk, chance, dullness,
leaving, being ignored, and disappearance as seed areas. It then shows a mixed
result: some candidates are useful, some are identities, some are cliches, and
some are rejected because they are grammatically or tonally poor.

For skill use, the output should show both candidates and labels:

```text
seed: ignored
useful: adored, bored, restored
watch: identity risk with same ending family
idea paths: neglect, craving attention, repair after neglect
```

The label is as important as the candidate. It teaches the writer to judge the
word, not just collect it.

## Chapter 7: full rhyme search

Chapter 7 returns to the worksheet after family rhyme, additive/subtractive
rhyme, assonance, consonance, and partial rhyme have been introduced. The
worksheet is no longer just a perfect-rhyme prewrite. It becomes a full search
across the stability scale in [rhyme types](rhyme-types.md), still filtered by
the song's idea.

The chapter's goal is not "more rhymes" in the abstract. It is better phrase-end
choices than perfect rhyme alone can provide.

> Rhymes are suggestive.

Use this chapter when the user has a lyric idea, a seed list, or a draft whose
line endings feel trapped by obvious perfect rhymes.

## Expand the worksheet after learning rhyme types

Keep the Chapter 3 worksheet frame: idea sketch -> central word list -> active search
-> filtering -> drafting. Chapter 7 adds a new middle layer:

```text
seed word
-> perfect rhymes
-> imperfect rhymes by relationship
-> idea-fit trimming
-> favorite candidate set
-> rewrite using the expanded sound map
```

For each seed, build two visible columns:

- Perfect rhymes: exact resolution, strong closure, high predictability.
- Imperfect rhymes: family, additive, subtractive, assonance, consonance, and
  partial options that can open fresher language.

Skill behavior: do not replace the earlier worksheet. Extend it. The user still
needs idea-fit labels, grammar warnings, identity warnings, and cliche warnings.
The new work is classifying and choosing from a wider sound field.

## Add high-value seeds before searching

Chapter 7 starts by adding `safe` and `business` to the worksheet from Chapter 3 because the
example lyric's core pressure is risky attraction versus safety. These are not
random keywords. They are phrase-end candidates tied to the title idea and the
emotional argument.

In skill use, add seeds from:

- The title or title phrase.
- The draft's current end words.
- Words that name the section's central conflict.
- Words whose opposites define the emotional pressure.
- Words that could become high-spotlight line endings.

If a user's existing draft says the important thing with weak phrase-end words,
make a better seed list before hunting rhymes.

## Search all rhyme relationships, then identify how they work

Chapter 7 asks the writer to figure out what kinds of rhymes the list contains and how
they were found. That makes the worksheet diagnostic. The writer should learn
which sound move created each candidate, not just copy the word.

Use the [rhyme types](rhyme-types.md) scale as the lookup reference, but keep the
worksheet focused on application:

- Perfect: use when a line needs full arrival or unmistakable closure.
- Family: swap consonants inside a phonetic family for a fresh but stable link.
- Additive: add a consonant after the shared sound when perfect rhyme is too
  closed.
- Subtractive: remove a consonant when the exact ending is too restrictive.
- Assonance: keep the vowel and let consonants move farther away.
- Consonance: keep consonant structure while changing the vowel.
- Partial: use a weaker sonic link when the structure needs movement, surprise,
  or less finality.

Skill behavior: label imperfect candidates by likely relationship where useful,
but do not overburden the user with a taxonomy lecture. The practical question
is, "What new usable line ending did this sound move open?"

## Keep the idea as the filter

The complete Chapter 7 search lists many candidates, but Pattison frames them as
related to the general "Risky Business" idea and says they will need trimming.
That is the governing rule: a wider rhyme field still has to serve the song.

Useful Chapter 7-style filters:

- Does this candidate fit the relationship, scene, and tone?
- Does it create a useful association the writer had not seen yet?
- Does it point toward a possible new line, image, or turn?
- Does it create accidental comedy?
- Is it an identity rather than a useful rhyme?
- Is it technically available but syntactically awkward?
- Is the pair too predictable or cliche?

Examples from the chapter's practice material:

- A seed can produce both perfect and imperfect options, and both should be
  judged by idea-fit.
- `left out` produces identity warnings in the perfect-rhyme column; mark them
  instead of treating them as ordinary rhymes.
- `chance` carries a cliche warning around an obvious perfect rhyme; do not let
  dictionary availability override listener expectation.
- `leave` includes an identity warning in the imperfect column; near sound does
  not automatically mean usable craft.

Skill behavior: when presenting a worksheet, include a `watch` or `reject`
category. This teaches the user why some technically valid rhymes should not be
used.

## Let rhymes suggest lyric direction

Chapter 7's key payoff is associative. Looking through a widened rhyme list can reveal
ideas that were not in the first prose sketch. The worksheet is a discovery
engine, not just a supply cabinet.

Coach the user to scan candidates for:

- A sharper emotional verb.
- A more concrete image.
- A turn from fear to desire, or desire to consequence.
- A stronger social or relational detail.
- A word that changes the speaker's level of confidence.
- A rhyme family that suggests a verse path distinct from the chorus path.

For example, a seed around being ignored can suggest attention, neglect, repair,
self-protection, or leaving. A seed around risk can suggest danger, appetite,
choice, blame, or escape. The worksheet should surface those paths before
drafting.

## Exercise 7.1: choose favorite perfect and imperfect rhymes

Exercise pattern to preserve:

- Start from the Chapter 7 keyword list.
- For each keyword, review both perfect and imperfect rhyme columns.
- Pick ten favorite rhymes for each keyword.
- Prefer favorites that fit the idea, not merely favorites that sound clever.
- Keep the selected words near the seed so they are easy to draft from.

Chapter 7 keyword set:

```text
scare, afraid, flirt, attention, left out, risk, chance, dull, leave, ignored,
gone, safe, business
```

Skill behavior: when a user asks to continue from a worksheet, ask them to pick
or approve a short favorite set before drafting. If they do not want to choose,
choose a balanced set: some stable, some surprising, all idea-fit.

## Exercise 7.2: rewrite from the expanded worksheet

Exercise pattern to preserve:

- Return to the lyric or section after the full search.
- Rewrite using some of the new idea paths and rhyme words.
- Do not force every selected candidate into the lyric.
- Let the expanded rhyme map change the available emotional and story moves.

For skill use, this becomes a coaching prompt:

```text
Rewrite this section using the expanded worksheet.
Keep the speaker, situation, and emotional pressure intact.
Use 3-6 selected rhyme candidates.
Avoid identities and marked cliches unless the line renews them.
Prefer one or two imperfect rhymes where perfect rhyme feels too closed.
```

The rewrite should sound less trapped than the original because the writer is no
longer choosing from one perfect-rhyme lane.

## Chapter 7 skill workflow

When applying Chapter 7 directly for a user:

1. Restate the lyric idea in one compact paragraph.
2. List 8-13 seeds, including title words and current phrase-end words.
3. For each important seed, generate perfect and imperfect candidates.
4. Label imperfect candidates by useful sound relationship when it helps.
5. Mark identities, cliches, grammar traps, and tone mismatches.
6. Choose a favorite set per seed or ask the user to choose.
7. Suggest two or three lyric directions opened by the candidate set.
8. Only then draft or rewrite the section.

Compact output example:

```text
seed: safe
perfect: faith, chase, space
imperfect: brave, grave, cage, stage, haste
watch: over-familiar safety/risk pairs
idea paths: caution, escape, public performance, locked-in fear

seed: business
perfect: none strong enough for default closure
imperfect: decisions, visions, forgiveness, suspicious, ambitious
watch: comic corporate tone unless wanted
idea paths: bad choices, imagined betrayal, forgiveness after risk
```

Do not dump every possible rhyme. Chapter 7 shows the full search as practice, but the
usable skill output should be a trimmed, labeled working map.

## Exercise 3.2: worksheet from a title

Exercise pattern to preserve:

- Take a short title.
- Put each important title word into the seed list.
- Add seven or more idea words from the sketch.
- Search each seed for idea-fitting rhyme candidates.

This exercise makes the title a generator instead of a fixed burden. If a title
word has weak rhyme potential, the worksheet reveals that early.

## Exercise 3.3: write sections from the worksheet

Exercise pattern to preserve:

- Use the worksheet to write two song sections, such as a verse and chorus.
- Use an original title or the chapter's risk/safety title idea.
- If using the provided title idea, do not put it in a rhyming position.

The last instruction matters: some titles are better as internal statements,
section openings, or refrain material. A title does not automatically belong at
line end.

Skill behavior: after generating a worksheet, offer to draft a section only
after the user picks a few candidate families. The worksheet should steer the
draft, not become decoration under it.

## English is rhyme-poor

Pattison closes the chapter by explaining why worksheets matter especially in
English. English does not use word endings systematically the way many Romance
languages do. Fewer shared endings means fewer obvious rhyme options, and many
of the available ones are overused.

Consequences:

- Common English rhyme pairs become cliches quickly.
- End positions are harder to use effectively.
- The writer needs a wider search strategy than perfect rhyme only.
- Later rhyme types are not luxuries; they solve a real language problem.

This prepares the move into family rhymes, relatives, cousins, and other rhyme
resources in later chapters.

## Skill output format

When `/songwriting:rhyme worksheet` generates a rhyme worksheet, prefer this structure:

```text
Idea sketch:
<one-paragraph prose focus>

Seed words:
1. <title or idea word> - why it matters
2. <title or idea word> - why it matters

Rhyme families:
<seed>
- strong candidates:
- usable with care:
- reject/watch:
- idea paths:

Drafting notes:
- best spotlight candidates:
- words to keep out of end-rhyme:
- likely cliches:
- possible verse direction:
- possible chorus direction:
```

Keep the worksheet compact enough to use while drafting. More candidates are
not automatically better; better-labeled candidates are better.
