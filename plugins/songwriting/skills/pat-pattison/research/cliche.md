# Cliche

Pat Pattison - *Writing Better Lyrics* (2009), Chapter 5.

Use this when a user asks why a lyric feels generic, predictable, sleepy,
familiar, or "AI-ish"; when a line leans on stock phrases, stock rhymes, stock
images, or stock metaphors; or when a draft needs brutal rewriting toward
specific sensory language.

## Core idea

Cliches are overused language units that once carried image or feeling but now
arrive pre-smoothed. They point toward a general territory instead of taking
the listener into a specific experience.

> Sense-bound is universal.

This is the central distinction: universal does not mean generic. Sense-bound
details let listeners fill the song with their own memories. Generic language
keeps them at a distance.

## Four cliche families

Diagnose cliches by family:

| Family | Symptom | Cure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Phrase | Familiar wording substitutes for real thought. | Ask what actually happened in body, room, object, or action. |
| Rhyme | Listener knows the next word before it arrives. | Use worksheet search and imperfect rhyme types. |
| Image | Stock objects stand in for lived detail. | Object-write into the exact setting and sensory evidence. |
| Metaphor | Dead comparisons carry no fresh pressure. | Build new collisions from [metaphor](metaphor.md). |

Do not fix cliche only by swapping a synonym. The problem is not vocabulary; it
is unfelt experience.

## Cliche phrases

Cliche phrases usually tell instead of show. They name a familiar emotion,
gesture, or relational situation without giving the listener a body, object,
room, weather, texture, or action to enter.

Diagnosis questions:

- Does this phrase appear in hundreds of songs?
- Could another singer in another story use it unchanged?
- Does it name a territory where an image should be?
- Does it explain emotion before showing Rusty's collar?
- Does it arrive because the writer is trying to fill meter or rhyme?

Rewrite pattern:

1. Underline the cliche phrase.
2. Translate it into the actual emotional job it is doing.
3. Ask what sensory event proves that feeling.
4. Object-write for two minutes on the phrase's real-life source.
5. Replace the phrase with a specific object, gesture, action, or scene.
6. Read the old and new lines aloud; keep the one that makes the listener
   participate.

## Cliche rhymes

Cliche rhymes are predictable pairings. They often appear because perfect rhyme
is the only tool being used. Predictability can drain the second line before it
is sung.

Use [worksheets](worksheets.md) to build options, then use
[rhyme types](rhyme-types.md) to widen the field:

- family rhyme for close-but-fresh resolution,
- additive or subtractive rhyme for loosened resolution,
- assonance when the line should stay open,
- consonance when the vowel should move but consonant connection can hold the
  structure.

Protect the idea first. If the obvious rhyme pulls the lyric into a stock
phrase, keep the idea and change the rhyme type.

## Cliche images

Cliche images are familiar objects that no longer show a specific life. The
Chapter 5 image inventory includes common body, romance, night, door, light, chain,
flower, phone, and dance-floor territory. These territories are not banned, but
they require personal detail to become active again.

Ask:

- Whose lips, eyes, hands, door, room, or car?
- What did the object actually look, smell, sound, or feel like?
- What was damaged, missing, stained, overheated, sticky, too bright, or too
  quiet?
- What exact action happened there?
- What detail could only come from this speaker?

If the user wants to use a common image, require a precise context.

## Cliche metaphors

Some metaphor worlds are heavily worn: weather for anger, fire for passion,
darkness for sadness or ignorance, seasons for life stages, prison for love,
walls for self-protection, and the broken heart family.

Do not ban these automatically. Ask whether the draft adds a fresh collision or
specific sensory angle. If it does not, rebuild the metaphor from:

- object-writing details,
- a less expected key of related words,
- a different grammatical form,
- verbal metaphor instead of adjective decoration,
- a concrete objective correlative.

## Friendly cliches

A cliche can work when the lyric creates a setup that restores or twists its
original meaning. The phrase must gain a second life from context. Without that
setup, treat it as a draft placeholder and replace it.

Use this test:

- Does the lyric make the familiar phrase mean something newly specific?
- Is the phrase carrying overtone rather than acting as the main idea?
- Would the line collapse if the cliche were removed?
- Has the song earned the phrase through image, situation, or dramatic irony?

If the answer is no, cut or rewrite.

## Draft-stage tolerance

Cliches are allowed in early drafts as markers. They can tell the writer what a
line is trying to do. They become a problem when mistaken for finished writing.

When coaching, do not shame the placeholder. Translate it:

```text
Placeholder: I can't live without you.
Job: dependency, fear, separation, physical panic.
Search: organic sense, daily objects, absent-person routines, room details.
Rewrite source: object writing and worksheet words.
```

## Chapter 5 exercise as coaching prompt

Exercise 10 - Cliche inventory and parody draft:

- Make a long personal list of cliche phrases, rhymes, images, and metaphors.
- Write a verse/chorus/verse/chorus draft using only those cliches.
- Notice how easily it fills space while saying little.
- Return to a real draft and mark any similar placeholders.
- Rewrite each one through sensory detail, fresh rhyme, or new metaphor.

## Skill workflow

When applying this file:

1. Scan the user's draft for phrase, rhyme, image, and metaphor cliches.
2. Mark each as placeholder, usable-with-setup, or must-rewrite.
3. For phrase cliches, use show-before-tell and object writing.
4. For rhyme cliches, use worksheet expansion and imperfect rhyme.
5. For image cliches, demand exact sensory ownership.
6. For metaphor cliches, generate new collisions.
7. Preserve a cliche only when the surrounding setup makes it newly active.
8. Recheck the rewrite for prosody so freshness does not break singability.
