## Core idea

Prosody is the appropriate relationship between elements. In songwriting, that
means every available element can either support the central intent or work
against it: words, melody, stressed syllables, stressed notes, rhythm, chords,
line count, line length, rhyme scheme, and rhyme type.

> Structure is your film score.

The film-score metaphor is practical: listeners may not consciously name the
structural cue, but they react to it. A lyric can say one thing while structure
quietly creates a contrary emotional signal underneath it.

Chapter 19 states the same operating principle more directly:

> Motion creates emotion.

The words carry meaning, but the section's motion carries feeling at the same
time. When both levels agree, the idea gets stronger. When they disagree on
purpose, the lyric can create irony, denial, suspense, or instability.

## Stable vs unstable

Start every structural choice with one question: is the section's emotional
state stable or unstable?

Stable conditions include resolution, confidence, commitment, factual clarity,
or emotional steadiness. Stable sections often benefit from even line counts,
matched line lengths, expected closures, clear rhyme placement, and stronger
rhyme types.

Unstable conditions include denial, loss, imbalance, incompleteness, anxiety,
irony, or unresolved desire. Unstable sections often benefit from odd line
counts, mismatched line lengths, withheld rhyme, weaker rhyme types, and
closures that leave an emotional edge.

Prosody does not mean every element must be unstable in an unstable section.
Sometimes one stable element makes the unstable element more audible. A regular
rhythm can make irregular line length feel sharper.

## Five structural elements

Every lyric section uses five structural elements:

1. Number of lines.
2. Length of lines.
3. Rhythm of lines.
4. Rhyme scheme.
5. Rhyme type.

Use these as a diagnostic grid. For each section, mark what the element is
doing emotionally before deciding whether to revise it.

## Motion notation

For structural motion, use capital letters to show lines that share all three
line-level features:

- Same rhyme sound.
- Same number of stressed syllables.
- Same basic rhythm.

Example: common meter can be marked `ABAB` when the first and third lines share
four stresses and the same rhyme, and the second and fourth lines share three
stresses and the same rhyme. If rhyme structure and line-length structure do
not align clearly, omit the capital-letter shorthand and mark the separate
features directly.

The shorthand is useful because it makes sequence visible. Once the ear hears
`AB`, then hears another `A`, it wants the matching `B`. Once it hears `ABC`,
then hears another `A` and `B`, it wants the matching `C`.

## Number of lines

Odd line counts tend to feel unresolved, incomplete, off-balance, or unstable.
Even line counts tend to feel balanced, complete, resolved, or stable.

Use odd line counts when the narrator, scene, or emotional claim should feel
unsettled. Use even line counts when the lyric should feel trustworthy,
grounded, or resolved.

Mismatch can create irony. If a narrator claims stability inside an unstable
line count, the structure can quietly undercut the claim.

## Length of lines

Line length is measured by stressed syllables, not raw syllables. Equal stress
lengths stop the ear; unequal stress lengths push the ear forward.

Prosody checks:

- Do two equal-length lines create a stopping place where the idea should stop?
- Does a shorter line make the section feel unfinished in a useful way?
- Does a run of equal lines establish stability before an unequal line disrupts
  it?
- Does the line-length pattern expose denial, longing, or imbalance beneath the
  surface statement?

## Rhythm of lines

Lyric rhythm and musical rhythm are not identical, but important stressed
syllables should land on important musical positions. This preserves the
natural shape of the language.

Regular lyric rhythm can create stability even when another structural element
creates instability. Use that contrast intentionally. If everything is irregular
at once, the listener may stop hearing the specific source of instability.

Rhythm is the first structural element the listener hears. A first line sets a
rhythmic and length standard before rhyme or total line count can be known.

Motion rules for rhythm:

- Regular rhythms create stable motion.
- Irregular rhythms create less stable motion.
- Matched rhythms across lines create stability.
- Unmatched rhythms across lines create instability.

## Rhyme scheme

Rhyme is a sonic guide. It shows connection, indicates stopping places, and
points the ear forward. A missing rhyme can make the listener feel lost; a
well-placed rhyme can create closure even before the listener understands why.

Use rhyme scheme as emotional navigation:

- Strongly paired rhymes can feel stable and resolved.
- A withheld rhyme can create forward motion.
- A delayed rhyme can pay off an expectation under a spotlight.
- A lack of rhyme can support a narrator or scene that feels lost.

## Rhyme type

Rhyme type controls resolution strength. Perfect rhyme is the most resolved;
family rhyme sits less resolved; additive/subtractive rhyme and assonance move
farther from resolution; consonance rhyme is least resolved.

Use weaker rhyme types when full closure would contradict the emotion. Use
stronger rhyme types when the section needs confidence, arrival, or trust.
Cross-reference `rhyme-types.md` for the detailed type definitions.

## Motion tools

Chapter 19 reduces motion to two interacting levels:

- Words and ideas create emotion through meaning.
- Overall lyric structure creates emotion through groupings and line structure.

Line structure combines rhythm, stressed-syllable length, and rhyme scheme.
Control those elements to make the section speed up, slow down, lean forward,
resolve, hang unresolved, or spotlight a specific word.

## Stability reference

Use the following as a practical stability map, not a formula. The same pattern
can change emotional effect when line lengths or rhyme types change.

Two-line sections:

- Most stable: matched line lengths with matched rhyme (`AA` / aa).
- Less stable: unmatched line lengths, especially longer-to-shorter.
- General rule: longer followed by shorter is less stable than shorter followed
  by longer.

Three-line sections:

- `AAA`: stable if the idea concludes; can still lean forward when repeated
  content asks for a fourth arrival.
- `ABB`: can close, but a long first line leans harder than a short first line.
- `XXX`: unstable because no rhyme or sequence guides the ear.
- `AAB`: tends to lean forward toward a matching `AABB` or `AABAAB`.
- `ABA`: creates clear expectation for another `B`.

Four-line sections:

- `AAAA`: very stable but can become static.
- `AABB`: stable couplet motion.
- `ABAB`: stable common-meter motion.
- `XAXA`: stable but more relaxed than `ABAB`.
- `ABAA`: deceptive closure; the final `A` fools the expected `B`.
- `XXAA`: a larger unit that closes only at the end.
- `XAAA`: stronger end pressure after an opening unmatched line.
- `AABA`: points forward toward another `AB`.
- `ABBA`: enclosed motion; can feel balanced but less direct.
- `AAAX`, `AXAX`, `XAAX`, and `XXXX`: use when floating or unstable motion
  supports the idea.

Five-line sections:

- One matching element: `AAAAA`, `XAAAA`, `XXAAA`, `XAXAA`, `XXXAA`,
  `XXAXA`, and `AAAAX` usually depend on how late the matching material arrives.
- Two matching elements: `ABABB`, `ABAAB`, `ABBAA`, `ABBAB`, `ABAAA`,
  `ABABA`, `AABBA`, `AABBB`, and `AAABB` create different balances of closure,
  sequence, and forward pull.
- Three matching elements: `ABCAC` and `ABCBC` create deceptive closure because
  the fifth line answers a line other than the expected one.
- `ABCBB`: the long `C` can reduce the fourth line's closure and shift the
  spotlight to line five.
- `ABCAA` and `ABCAB`: tend to push forward toward a missing `C`.

Six-line sections:

- `ABCABC`: strong sequence; moves forward to a satisfying line-six resolution.
- `ABABAA` and `ABABCC`: close after line four, then finish with a stabilizing
  couplet.
- `AABAAB`: stable, but the opening couplet slows the sequence.
- `ABABAB`: mostly stable, with slight forward curiosity about whether another
  `AB` will follow.
- `ABBABB`: can feel stable but may float if sequence is weak.
- `ABABBA`: two unexpected closures in reverse order; useful for surprise.

## Sequence

Sequence is expectation created by partial repetition of a structural pattern.
`ABCAB` wants `C` because the ear has learned the pattern. This can overpower
the raw number of lines: a five-line section can feel incomplete if the sequence
points toward a sixth line.

Sequence coaching checks:

- What pattern has the section taught the ear?
- Which line would complete the pattern?
- Does withholding that line create useful tension or merely incompletion?
- Is the title placed where the sequence creates the strongest spotlight?
- Would a shorter or longer version of one line change the stability enough to
  support the lyric's attitude?

## Analysis workflow

1. Identify the section's emotional claim.
2. Label the claim stable, unstable, or intentionally mixed.
3. Count the number of lines.
4. Count stressed syllables per line.
5. Mark lyric rhythm and note where it is regular or irregular.
6. Mark the rhyme scheme.
7. Mark the rhyme type at each rhyme point.
8. Mark A/B/C motion notation only where line length, rhythm, and rhyme align.
9. Ask what sequence the section has created.
10. Ask whether each element supports, undercuts, or complicates the claim.
11. Revise the element with the biggest mismatch first.

## Coaching prompts

- Film-score audit: choose a draft section and name the emotion that the
  structure creates before rereading the words. Then compare that emotion with
  the stated meaning.
- Stable/unstable rewrite: write the same four-to-six-line idea once with a
  stable structure and once with an unstable structure. Keep the words as close
  as possible so the structural difference is audible.
- Denial test: put a confident narrator inside an unstable structure. Use odd
  line count, short final lines, or weaker rhyme to let the listener know the
  narrator is wrong.
- Rhyme-color pass: replace a perfect rhyme with a family, assonance, or
  consonance rhyme and listen for how much resolution disappears.
- Two-line stability pass: write matched and unmatched two-line sections; test
  shorter-to-longer and longer-to-shorter versions.
- Three-line motion pass: write `AAA`, `ABB`, `AAB`, and `ABA`; for `AAA`, try
  resolved and unresolved ideas; for `ABB`, compare long-first-line and
  short-first-line versions.
- Four-line reference pass: draft `AAAA`, `AABB`, `ABAB`, `XAXA`, `ABAA`,
  `XXAA`, `XAAA`, `AABA`, `ABBA`, `AAAX`, `AXAX`, `XAAX`, and `XXXX`, naming
  where the ear stops and where it leans forward.
- Five-line title targeting: choose `ABCAC` or `ABCBC`, then build a title from
  the unmatched line by matching both rhythm and rhyme; target an inner vowel
  rather than only the end rhyme.
- Five-line shortened-line test: shorten the `C` line in `ABCBB` or `ABCAA`
  and describe how motion changes.
- Six-line sequence pass: rewrite `AABAAB` with `B` shortened to three
  stresses, then compare how solid or forward-leaning the section feels.

## Revision moves

- Match stable emotions with evenness, balance, and stronger closure.
- Match unstable emotions with oddness, imbalance, withholding, and weaker
  closure.
- Let one stable element sharpen a chosen unstable element.
- Use line count, line length, rhythm, rhyme scheme, and rhyme type as separate
  knobs.
- Use structural mismatch deliberately for irony, not by accident.
- Use A/B/C notation to expose sequence.
- Use sequence to create forward motion and title spotlights.
- Let line length, rhyme scheme, and line count disagree only when the emotional
  effect is worth the instability.

## Motion creates e-motion — four controllers

Pat's American Songwriter column condenses the engine into four controllers
that together create the song's motion (and therefore its emotion):

1. **Rhyme scheme** — pace, flow, closure.
2. **Musical groove** — the rhythmic feel under the lyric.
3. **Harmonic rhythm** — how often chords change.
4. **Melodic rhythm** — long notes vs short notes, syncopation, line-end
   note durations.

> "Motion creates e-motion. How you make it move, all by itself, creates
> a feeling." — Pat

The lyric controller (this skill's domain) is the rhyme-scheme one. The
other three live in the music. The lyric's motion choices have to fit the
musical controllers, push against them deliberately, or expose a mismatch.

See [stable / unstable](stable-unstable-meta.md) for the cross-controller
diagnostic.

## Greedy spots — the anti-pattern

A greedy spot is a stressed syllable that lands on a weak musical beat, or
an unstressed syllable forced onto a strong beat. The mismatch steals
emphasis from where the music wants it, distorts the natural shape of the
words, and breaks the listener's parse of the meaning.

Causes:

- The lyric's stressed syllable falls between the bar's strong beats.
- A multi-syllable word is set so its primary stress lands off the
  downbeat.
- A grammar word (article, preposition, conjunction) is forced onto a
  strong beat for rhythmic convenience.
- The melody was written first and the lyric was set without
  re-scanning natural stress against the bar.

Diagnosis:

1. Sing the line at performance tempo.
2. Mark every musical strong beat (downbeats, beat 3 in 4/4).
3. Mark every word-level stressed syllable.
4. Where they disagree, you have a greedy spot.

Fixes:

- Rewrite the line so the stressed syllables shift onto strong beats.
- Choose a synonym whose primary stress falls in the right position.
- Move a word earlier or later in the line.
- Reshape the line's grammar so the important word lands first or last.
- If the melody is the hook, the lyric must yield. If the lyric is the
  hook, change the melody if possible. See
  [lyric-melodic roadmaps](lyric-melodic-roadmaps.md) "three fixes".

Greedy spots are usually a symptom of a larger roadmap mismatch. Fixing
one greedy spot often fixes adjacent ones.

## Ordinary-language preservation

Pat returns repeatedly to a single rule: words have meaning because
they have ordinary uses. Distorting natural word stress for musical
convenience drains meaning.

> "Preserve the natural shape of the language." — Pat
> (patpattison.com)

Two corollaries:

1. **Singing a wrong stress changes what the word seems to mean.** The
   listener's parse runs first on stress, then on dictionary definition.
   Forcing stress against the word's nature produces ambiguity the
   writer did not intend.
2. **A line that does not sound like speech, sounds like a song trying
   too hard.** Speak the line at conversational pace. If you have to
   contort it to scan, the music must give way, or the line must change.

The exception: deliberate distortion is a tool. Forcing a wrong stress
on the title's final syllable can make the title sound suspended,
unsettled, or sneering. The choice is conscious. Most greedy spots are
not.

## Tone of voice as a stability lever

Pat extends the stable/unstable diagnostic with a non-rhyme, non-meter
axis: tone of voice. A line printed on the page has one stability
character; the same line sung in two different voices has two.

Levers carried by tone:

- Volume — soft is less stable; loud is more stable for confidence,
  less stable for desperation.
- Attack — clipped attack is stable, decisive; smeared attack is
  unstable, hesitant.
- Pitch direction — rising endings open; falling endings close.
- Breathiness — breath leakage is unstable, intimate; clean tone is
  stable, declarative.
- Vibrato — held straight tone is stable, frozen; wide vibrato is
  unstable, longing.
- Tempo of phrasing — pushed against the beat is unstable; sitting
  with the beat is stable.

The skill cannot coach the singer, but the writer must choose lyric
content that the chosen tone can carry without contradiction. A
declarative chorus delivered breathy from below the beat undermines
the words, on purpose or by accident.

> "Tone of voice can change stability without changing a single
> word." — paraphrase of Berklee article

If a draft "sounds wrong" but the printed page looks fine, the tone
of voice may be the lever doing the work. Sing the chorus two ways.

## Origin note — *Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure* (1991), Chapter 2

Pat first introduces the term **prosody** in *Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure* (1991), Chapter 2, originally with a
narrower meaning: phrase length fitting the lyric's meaning. Across Books 2
and 4 and the Berklee Online / Coursera courses, the term expanded to
cover all five compositional elements + melody + harmony + melodic rhythm
— "structure is your film score."

The expansion matters: the 1991 meaning is **phrase pacing matches idea
pacing**; the modern meaning is **all structural surfaces support the
emotion**. Both are valid; the narrower meaning is a subset of the broader
one.

When reading this file, the broader meaning is in effect. The narrower
1991 meaning surfaces specifically in `phrasing.md`'s phrase-length
discussion.

## Three phrasing types (Berklee Online OSONG-525)

Beyond the front-heavy / back-heavy frame in `phrasing.md`, Pat's
graduate-level course adds a third type:

1. **Front-heavy** — stress lands early in the bar (on or near downbeat).
   Anchored feeling. Forward motion suppressed by the early lock.
2. **Back-heavy** — stress lands late in the bar (after the downbeat).
   Forward-leaning feeling. Motion preserved.
3. **Strong/weak bar** — a phrase that spans two bars where one bar is
   stress-heavy and the other is stress-light. Creates a strong/weak
   alternation at the bar level, not within a single bar.

The third type is harder to perceive without sight-reading musical
notation — it requires hearing the bar structure clearly. When the melody
makes the bar boundaries audible, strong/weak bar phrasing creates
section-internal motion the other two types can't access alone.

Use the third type when:

- a section needs internal motion without rhyme-density change
- a chorus needs alternating weight without changing line lengths
- a melody already establishes strong bars and the lyric needs to match
