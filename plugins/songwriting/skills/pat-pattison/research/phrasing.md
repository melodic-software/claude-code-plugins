# Phrasing

Pat Pattison - *Songwriting: Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure* (1991), Chapter 1-2;
extended via patpattison.com "Art of Phrasing" and "Lyric and Melodic Phrases"
articles for front-heavy/back-heavy framing and body-language metaphor.

## Core idea

Phrasing controls how lyric thought-units line up with musical thought-units,
then uses count and length to manage balance, speed, motion, and prosody.

Pattison treats "phrase" broadly: a sentence, a clause, or a natural
sentence-piece that has enough identity to be heard as a unit.

> "Phrases are sentences or natural pieces of sentences sometimes called 'clauses.'"

A phrase does not need to be grammatically complete, but it must feel complete
enough to carry weight. "Who scheme and run wild" can work as a dependent
phrase; "who scheme and" cannot, because the ear waits for completion.

## Phrase identity

Lyric phrases can be nested. A short phrase can be a real phrase and also part
of a longer phrase. Count both levels when diagnosing a section:

```text
short phrase / short phrase
longer phrase made from the two short phrases
```

The test is whether the unit has a natural identity in speech. Some short
phrases stand alone. Others depend on a larger sentence but still feel shaped.
Fragments that clearly need missing words are not useful phrase units.

For grammar-heavy passages, slash the sentence where natural phrase boundaries
fall. Pattison's Thoreau exercise matters because it trains the ear to hear
phrase boundaries before the lyric is set to music.

## Lyric phrases and musical phrases

Lyric phrases and musical phrases should usually match. If the music stops in
the middle of a natural language unit, the listener hears the mismatch instead
of the emotion.

Mark musical phrases with brackets and lyric phrases with slashes:

```text
[lyric phrase / lyric phrase /]
[one musical phrase continuing]
```

Do not let the music make a partial thought sound complete. A musical phrase
boundary can temporarily change meaning before the lyric corrects it. When a
line can mean either "I know your plans" or "I know your plans do not include
me," phrase boundaries decide which thought the listener hears first.

## Counting phrases

Count phrases at two levels:

- Short phrases: the smallest natural lyric units.
- Long phrases: combinations that form larger musical or syntactic units.

Both levels matter. Pattison's Steely Dan example can be heard as eight short
phrases or four longer phrases; either count feels balanced because it is even.

## Balance and unbalance

Even phrase counts feel balanced. Odd phrase counts feel unbalanced.

Balanced sections tend to stop motion. Unbalanced sections create motion because
the listener expects a balancing phrase that has not arrived.

Use that deliberately:

- Balance to land an idea.
- Unbalance to push into the next section.
- Change the balance pattern to contrast sections.

## Spotlight the important idea

The last phrase in a balanced section is a stopping position, so it naturally
spotlights whatever lands there. Put summary lines, reversals, and emotional
turns in that position when they should carry weight.

Worked example: in Buck Ram's "The Great Pretender," the fourth phrase lands
the section's emotional summary. The even-numbered position makes the line feel
like the point of the section, not just another detail.

Revision move: if the final phrase of a balanced section is not the main idea,
try rearranging the same phrases so the important idea lands last.

## Create forward motion

An odd number of phrases withholds the expected stop. This can build pressure
before a chorus or in a transitional section.

Worked example: Pattison shows a three-phrase build made from repeated short
requests. The repeated wording is less important than the count: three units
imply a fourth, so the section leans forward when the fourth never arrives.

Pattern:

```text
Phrase A
Phrase A repeated or varied
Phrase A intensified
```

The same effect can happen with three different phrases if they share a clear
shape. The listener still wants a balancing position.

## Contrast sections

Balance can change across verses. A first verse can be fully balanced so it
settles; a second verse can add one extra phrase so it pushes into the chorus.

Worked example: Pattison points to Kevin Cronin's "Can't Fight This Feeling."
The first verse balances. The second verse adds a phrase near the end, tipping
the structure forward and making the chorus feel like the needed landing point.

This also works inside a repeated verse form: an extra final phrase can surprise
the ear, spotlight the normal balanced position, and then spotlight the added
line even more strongly.

## Phrase length controls speed

After phrase count controls balance, phrase length controls speed. The goal is
control, not speed for its own sake.

Shorter-than-established phrases accelerate. Longer-than-established phrases
decelerate. Equal phrase lengths keep the pace constant.

This only works by contrast. A phrase is fast or slow relative to the pace
already established.

## Acceleration

Shorter phrases compress time. In a limerick, the shorter third and fourth
phrases speed the structure before the punchline. In a song section, shorter
phrases can sound active, urgent, comic, breathless, or cornered.

Worked example: Pattison's discussion of Beth Nielsen Chapman's "Years" points
to a short final phrase about time moving quickly. The phrase length enacts the
idea: the structure speeds up exactly where the lyric says time does.

Use acceleration when the lyric should feel impatient, physically active,
compressed, comic, or pushed toward a hook.

## Deceleration

Longer phrases stretch time. They can slow the section, make a thought feel
heavy, or make an important phrase hang over the established pattern.

Worked example: in Gary Nicholson and John Jarvis's "Fathers and Sons,"
Pattison notes that the title-bearing final phrase is much longer than the
nearby short phrases. The long phrase decelerates dramatically and spotlights
the title idea.

Use deceleration when the lyric should feel reflective, emotionally weighted,
expansive, delayed, or like a landing point.

## Contrasting sections by length

Section-to-section phrase length can create prosodic contrast:

- Short chorus phrases after longer verse phrases can feel more active, snug,
  bodily, or hook-driven.
- Longer chorus phrases after a fast verse can feel relaxed, nostalgic, or
  settled.
- A verse with intellectual or explanatory ideas may need longer phrases; a
  chorus with physical or emotional action may need shorter phrases.

The point is not "short = good" or "long = good." The phrase length should
behave like the idea.

Worked example, verse-long to chorus-short. Pattison's "Skin Tight" verse uses
longer phrases ("Lovin' ain't no fashion show / Changing all the time...");
the chorus drops to short, tightly packed phrases ("SKIN TIGHT / Fits right /
Nice and snug"). The chorus is the snug section; its phrase length enacts the
title.

Worked example, longer-verse intellectual to shorter-chorus active. Pattison's
"Ready or Not" verse is built from long explanatory phrases ("If I went into
analysis / And took myself apart / And laid me out for both of us to see");
the chorus answers with short, decisive phrases ("READY OR NOT / We've got
what we've got / Let's give it a shot"). The contrast moves from
self-examination into commitment.

Worked example, reverse direction. Randy Newman's "My Old Kentucky Home" runs
short fast phrases in the verse ("Turpentine and dandelion wine / I've turned
the corner and I'm doing fine"); the chorus stretches into longer phrases
that "lean back on the haystack" ("Oh the sun shines bright on MY OLD
KENTUCKY HOME"). Same principle, opposite direction: verse is busy and comic;
chorus is settled and porch-bound.

## Prosody

Pattison uses "prosody" for structural fit: meaning and form working together
instead of merely coexisting.

> "Prosody means that things fit well with one another."

Phrase count creates stop or motion. Phrase length creates acceleration or
deceleration. Together, they let the structure act out the lyric's meaning.

The "Fathers and Sons" discussion shows both balls in motion: short phrases
make quoted advice feel clipped, an added phrase throws the second verse off
balance, and a long title phrase slows the ending so the title gets attention.

## Practice method

For any draft section:

1. Slash the natural lyric phrase boundaries.
2. Bracket the musical phrase boundaries.
3. Count short phrase units.
4. Count larger phrase groups.
5. Mark which position currently spotlights the strongest idea.
6. Label phrase lengths as constant, accelerating, or decelerating.
7. Decide whether the section should stop, lean forward, speed up, or slow down.

## Revision moves

When a section feels wrong, test phrase structure before rewriting every line:

- Move the strongest idea into a balanced stopping position.
- Add a phrase to unbalance a section and push forward.
- Remove a phrase to restore balance and stop motion.
- Shorten phrases to accelerate.
- Lengthen phrases to decelerate.
- Match lyric phrase boundaries to musical phrase boundaries.
- Contrast verse and chorus phrase lengths when the ideas contrast.

## Clause taxonomy

When slashing a lyric for phrase boundaries, name the kind of clause each
unit is. Different clause types feel different in song:

| Clause type | Example shape | Sonic effect |
|---|---|---|
| Prepositional | "in the rain", "under the bridge" | Setting; locates the listener |
| Verbal | "running home", "having said that" | Action without subject; pushes forward |
| Adjective | "wearing the same coat", "tired and alone" | Modifier; colors the noun |
| Adverb | "long after midnight", "without warning" | Modifier; colors the verb |
| Noun (subject or object) | "the longest day", "what you meant" | The thing itself; stabilizes |
| Independent clause | "she said goodbye" | Full thought; stops |
| Dependent clause | "who left the door open" | Hangs; requires anchor |

Use the taxonomy to diagnose why a section feels jerky: too many
independent clauses can feel choppy (every line a stop); too many
dependent clauses can feel suspended (nothing lands). Mix clause types
for natural sentence motion in song.

> "Phrases are sentences or natural pieces of sentences sometimes
> called 'clauses.'" — Pat (*Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure* (1991), Chapter 1)

## Front-heavy and back-heavy phrases

Where a lyric phrase begins relative to the bar shapes its emotional
character. Two states:

- **Front-heavy** — the phrase begins on or picks up to the downbeat.
  The first stressed syllable lands on or just after beat 1. The phrase
  feels anchored, settled, stable. Body language: standing still.
- **Back-heavy** — the phrase begins after the downbeat (often on
  beat 2, beat 3, or an "and"). The first stressed syllable arrives
  late in the bar. The phrase feels in motion, unsettled, leaning
  forward. Body language: walking, falling, reaching.

> "Front-heavy phrases feel solid, stable. They are anchored." — Pat
> (patpattison.com)

> "Back-heavy phrases are in motion — they feel unstable." — Pat

Strong vs weak bars amplify the effect. In a 4-bar or 8-bar grouping,
bars alternate strong (1, 3, 5, 7) and weak (2, 4, 6, 8). Pairings:

| Phrase position | Bar strength | Combined feel |
|---|---|---|
| Front-heavy | Strong bar | Most stable; arrival, declaration |
| Front-heavy | Weak bar | Stable but setup-shaped |
| Back-heavy | Strong bar | Tension; leaning toward the next strong bar |
| Back-heavy | Weak bar | Most unstable; falling forward |

Use the pairings to plan section motion before drafting words. A chorus
arrival often wants front-heavy on a strong bar. A verse pickup often
wants back-heavy on a weak bar.

## Body language

Pat reframes phrasing as the song's body language. Where a phrase sits
against the bar communicates emotion before the words register.

> "Phrasing has the power to create emotion. It's the body language."
> — Pat (patpattison.com)

> "Line like body language helps deliver the emotion you intend."
> — Pat (seminar copy)

Three diagnostic questions:

1. What does this phrase's body language say — standing still, walking,
   reaching, falling, leaning, pivoting?
2. Does the body language match the lyric's emotion?
3. If you swapped front-heavy to back-heavy (or vice versa), would the
   line still mean what you want?

The body-language framing is especially useful when coaching a co-writer
or singer who needs an emotional handle before a technical one. "Make
this line walk forward" lands faster than "make this line back-heavy".

## Coaching prompts (extended)

- Speak the line at conversational pace. Where does the first stressed
  syllable land relative to the bar?
- Mark each phrase in the section as front-heavy or back-heavy. Pair
  each with strong vs weak bar.
- Where does the pattern create motion? Where does it stop?
- If the section feels static, are too many phrases front-heavy on
  strong bars?
- If the section feels chaotic, are too many phrases back-heavy on
  weak bars?
- Name the body language of each section in one phrase ("walking
  away", "standing still", "falling forward"). Does the printed lyric
  agree?

## Three-use balance taxonomy (*Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure* (1991), Chapter 1)

Pat's *Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure* (1991), Chapter 1 lists three deliberate uses for balanced and unbalanced
phrase counts. The concept is present elsewhere in the skill but the
named three-use taxonomy is *Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure* (1991)'s distinctive framing:

1. **Spotlight** — use balance/unbalance to draw attention to a specific
   line or section. The balance shift IS the spotlight. A section that
   was balanced suddenly becoming unbalanced makes the listener notice
   the change.
2. **Push sections forward** — use unbalance to refuse closure. An odd
   phrase count leaves the listener wanting more; that want IS the
   forward push. Balance closes; imbalance opens.
3. **Contrast one section with another** — use different balance
   patterns in adjacent sections to make the sections feel structurally
   distinct. A balanced verse + unbalanced bridge contrasts on the
   balance axis itself, independent of content.

Pat uses "The Great Pretender" (*Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure* (1991), Chapter 1) as an example where adding
one extra phrase to a section spotlights TWO lines more powerfully than
the extra phrase alone — the spotlight effect is multiplicative, not
additive.

The three uses are not exclusive. A single balance choice can do all
three at once (spotlight a specific line, push the section forward, and
contrast with the surrounding sections). Choose the use(s) deliberately
when picking phrase counts.
